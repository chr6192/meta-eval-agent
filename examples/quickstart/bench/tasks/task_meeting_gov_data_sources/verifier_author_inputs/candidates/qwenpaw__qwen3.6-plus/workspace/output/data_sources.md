## Detailed Source Entries

---

### Government/Military Sensors

#### 1. DOD/IC Military Sensors (General)
- **Owner/Operator:** U.S. Department of Defense (DOD) / Intelligence Community (IC)
- **Description:** Broad category of military and intelligence sensors used for surveillance, targeting, and reconnaissance across all domains (air, space, maritime).
- **Relevance to UAP:** Sean Kirkpatrick noted these provide the bulk of AARO's UAP data holdings. However, they are designed for military targeting — "to identify an object that is known and put a weapon on it" — not for scientific observation.
- **Limitations:** Not scientifically calibrated; designed for known-target identification, not anomaly detection; data is often classified due to the sensor platform, not the observed phenomenon; limited coverage of non-threat areas; restricted by legal constraints over U.S. territory.
- **Referenced by:** Sean Kirkpatrick, David Spergel

#### 2. F-35 EO/IR Camera Sensors
- **Owner/Operator:** U.S. Department of Defense
- **Description:** Electro-optical/infrared imaging sensors mounted on F-35 fighter jets, providing high-resolution imagery of airborne targets.
- **Relevance to UAP:** David Spergel used the F-35 camera as an example of how even benign subjects (e.g., a bird) photographed by classified sensors produce classified data, limiting scientific analysis.
- **Limitations:** Data is classified; sensors are not optimized for scientific data collection; subject to ghosting, optical artifacts, and uncalibrated conditions; performance varies with sun angle, altitude, speed, and lighting.
- **Referenced by:** Sean Kirkpatrick, David Spergel

#### 3. MQ-9 Electro-Optical (EO) Sensor
- **Owner/Operator:** U.S. Department of Defense
- **Description:** EO imaging sensor on the MQ-9 Reaper unmanned aerial vehicle, used for persistent surveillance.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Sean Kirkpatrick presented declassified footage from an MQ-9 EO sensor showing a spherical metallic orb in the Middle East (2022). Walter Scott and Josh Sumeta discussed this as an example of a well-understood sensor producing data on a confirmed real object.
- **Limitations:** Single-sensor observations lack independent corroboration; gimbal motion can create apparent target movement; sensor artifacts and processing effects need careful analysis.
- **Referenced by:** Sean Kirkpatrick, Walter Scott, Josh Sumeta

#### 4. P-3 Orion Sensor Suite
- **Owner/Operator:** U.S. Navy / DOD
- **Description:** Sensor payload on the P-3 maritime patrol aircraft, including EO/IR cameras and radar.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Kirkpatrick showed newly declassified P-3 footage of three apparent UAP objects that were later matched to commercial aircraft via air traffic control data, demonstrating how sensor jitter and parallax create misleading perceptions.
- **Limitations:** Sensor platform motion causes jitter/parallax effects; distance estimation errors create misperception of object speed and behavior; gimbal stabilization may or may not be active.
- **Referenced by:** Sean Kirkpatrick

#### 5. AARO Purpose-Built Dedicated Sensors
- **Owner/Operator:** AARO / DOD
- **Description:** Custom-designed sensors purpose-built by AARO to detect, track, and characterize UAP objects based on the "typical target package" profile (orb morphology, 1–4 m size, 0 to Mach 2 speed).
- **Relevance to UAP:** Represents AARO's shift from relying on existing military sensors to deploying dedicated UAP detection infrastructure. Will be placed in select hotspot areas for 24/7 surveillance campaigns.
- **Limitations:** Still in bootstrap/early deployment phase; only covers limited geographic areas; still being evaluated against the target profile.
- **Referenced by:** Sean Kirkpatrick

#### 6. DOD Gimbaled Sensor Systems (FLIR/EO)
- **Owner/Operator:** U.S. Navy / DOD
- **Description:** Gimbal-mounted electro-optical and forward-looking infrared cameras used on naval aircraft. The "Go Fast" Navy video was captured using such a system, with on-screen telemetry including elevation angle, azimuth, target range, aircraft altitude, airspeed, and time.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Josh Sumeta demonstrated how the telemetry data on these displays allows quantitative analysis — calculating the object's altitude (~13,000 ft) and true velocity (~40 mph, consistent with wind), debunking the impression of high-speed movement.
- **Limitations:** Gimbal jitter introduces apparent motion; range data may have uncertainties; single-sensor perspective creates parallax illusions; cognitive biases affect operator interpretation.
- **Referenced by:** Josh Sumeta, Sean Kirkpatrick

### Civilian Aviation Systems

#### 7. FAA Short-Range Radars (ASR — Airport Surveillance Radar)
- **Owner/Operator:** FAA
- **Description:** Ground-based radar systems located at high- and medium-density airports. Detection range: 40–60 nautical miles; altitude coverage: up to ~24,000 feet.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Mike Freie presented radar coverage maps showing ASR detection capability for a 1 m² target at various altitudes (1,000 ft, 10,000 ft, etc.). Useful for understanding what the FAA can and cannot detect in civil airspace.
- **Limitations:** Detection depends heavily on target size (radar cross-section); a small UAS (~0.01 m²) is 100× harder to detect than a 4th-gen fighter (~1 m²); coverage gaps in the western U.S. due to terrain and fewer airports; filtering settings deliberately remove biologicals, insects, dust, and ground clutter.
- **Referenced by:** Mike Freie

#### 8. FAA Long-Range Radars (ARSR-4 — Air Route Surveillance Radar)
- **Owner/Operator:** FAA
- **Description:** Long-range ground-based radar systems located around the perimeter of the continental U.S., equally spaced. Detection range: 200–250 nautical miles; altitude coverage: up to 100,000 feet.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Provides en-route and homeland defense surveillance coverage at higher altitudes across the U.S.
- **Limitations:** Line-of-sight limited by Earth curvature; geographical screening from mountains (especially in western U.S.); fixed-face rotating at 5 or 12 RPM — not taskable or steerable; data is processed/filtered, not raw.
- **Referenced by:** Mike Freie

#### 9. FAA Long-Range Radars (CRSR — Common Remote Sensor Replacement)
- **Owner/Operator:** FAA / DOD (shared mission use)
- **Description:** Interior U.S. long-range radar systems used for both air traffic control and national defense/homeland security missions.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Provides coverage in the interior of the U.S., complementing the perimeter-based ARSR-4 systems.
- **Limitations:** Same as ARSR-4 — fixed rotation, filtered data, terrain-dependent coverage.
- **Referenced by:** Mike Freie

#### 10. ADS-B (Automatic Dependent Surveillance-Broadcast)
- **Owner/Operator:** FAA (relies on cooperative avionics onboard aircraft)
- **Description:** Cooperative surveillance system where aircraft broadcast their GPS-derived position, altitude, speed, and identity. Preferred data source for airspace management.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Provides excellent coverage across the U.S. down to 1,500 feet AGL for equipped aircraft. However, it is a cooperative system — it only detects aircraft that are actively broadcasting, so it cannot detect unknown or non-cooperative objects.
- **Limitations:** Cannot detect non-equipped, non-cooperative, or intentionally non-broadcasting objects; not useful for detecting UAP that do not carry ADS-B transponders.
- **Referenced by:** Mike Freie

#### 11. FAA Surface Surveillance Systems
- **Owner/Operator:** FAA
- **Description:** Ground-based sensor systems for monitoring aircraft and vehicle movement on airport surfaces.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Mentioned for completeness; not directly applicable to aerial UAP detection.
- **Limitations:** Limited to airport surface operations only.
- **Referenced by:** Mike Freie

#### 12. FAA ERAM System (En Route Automation Modernization)
- **Owner/Operator:** FAA
- **Description:** The air traffic control automation system used in en-route centers for processing and displaying radar data.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Mike Freie noted that retained radar data is in the processed form as displayed on ERAM (and STAR) systems, not raw radar returns.
- **Limitations:** Retains processed/filtered data, not raw INQ data; raw data collection would be cost-prohibitive due to massive data volumes.
- **Referenced by:** Mike Freie

#### 13. FAA STAR System (Standard Terminal Automation Replacement)
- **Owner/Operator:** FAA
- **Description:** Terminal-area air traffic control automation system for processing and displaying radar data at approach control facilities.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Same as ERAM — displays processed radar data to controllers; retained data is from the display output, not raw sensor data.
- **Limitations:** Same as ERAM.
- **Referenced by:** Mike Freie

#### 14. FAA Domestic Event Network (UAP Reports)
- **Owner/Operator:** FAA
- **Description:** FAA organizational function where air traffic controllers report UAP sightings/events. Reports are archived.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Historically receives 3–5 UAP reports per month from controllers across the entire U.S. Saw upticks in August 2022 (~8–10 reports, possibly Starlink-related) and February 2023 (68 reports following the Chinese balloon incident).
- **Limitations:** Very low reporting rate relative to 45,000 daily flights and 14,000 controllers; reports are from controllers, not pilots; it is unclear whether these reports feed into AARO's database.
- **Referenced by:** Mike Freie, Mike Gold

#### 15. FAA ATC Towers (520 towers)
- **Owner/Operator:** FAA
- **Description:** 520 air traffic control towers at the highest-density airports across the U.S.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Part of the FAA surveillance infrastructure; controllers at towers can observe and report anomalous events.
- **Limitations:** Visual observation only (no sensor data); limited to airport vicinity.
- **Referenced by:** Mike Freie

#### 16. FAA TRACONs (147 terminal radar approach controls)
- **Owner/Operator:** FAA
- **Description:** 147 manned radar control facilities at high- and medium-density airports, managing terminal-area air traffic.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Part of the broader FAA surveillance network covering terminal airspace.
- **Limitations:** Limited to terminal airspace coverage.
- **Referenced by:** Mike Freie

#### 17. FAA ARTCCs (21 Air Route Traffic Control Centers)
- **Owner/Operator:** FAA
- **Description:** 21 en-route centers managing air traffic in the high-altitude cruise phase of flight across the U.S.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Provides surveillance coverage of en-route airspace at higher altitudes where long-range radars operate.
- **Limitations:** Relies on the same radar infrastructure as above.
- **Referenced by:** Mike Freie

#### 18. Drone Registry / UAS Tracking Data (Part 107)
- **Owner/Operator:** FAA
- **Description:** Registry of 880,000+ registered small drones (Part 107) and an additional ~1.69 million recreational/model small UAS.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Nadia Drake cited these numbers as part of the "background noise" — a massive number of uncontrolled aerial objects that complicate UAP identification. Mike Freie noted drones are a significant challenge for detection due to their small radar cross-section (~0.01 m²).
- **Limitations:** Many private drone operators are untracked; drones are regulated to fly below 400 feet but compliance is variable; small size makes radar detection difficult.
- **Referenced by:** Mike Freie, Nadia Drake

### Space-Based Assets

#### 19. NASA Earth Sensing Satellites
- **Owner/Operator:** NASA
- **Description:** NASA's fleet of Earth observation satellites collecting data on atmospheric, oceanic, terrestrial, and environmental phenomena across multiple spectral bands.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Sean Kirkpatrick specifically recommended evaluating whether Earth sensing satellites could detect UAP-like objects. Panelists discussed leveraging these for correlative analysis of environmental conditions during UAP events.
- **Limitations:** Not designed or calibrated for UAP detection; temporal resolution (frame rate) may be too slow for fast-moving objects; spatial resolution may not resolve small objects; data is collected for specific science objectives, not open surveillance.
- **Referenced by:** Sean Kirkpatrick, Nicola Fox, Paula Bontempi

#### 20. NASA Radiological Sensors (Space-Based)
- **Owner/Operator:** NASA
- **Description:** Space-based instruments for detecting and measuring radiation in Earth's environment and beyond.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Sean Kirkpatrick mentioned these as a NASA capability that could assist in UAP analysis, potentially detecting anomalous radiation signatures.
- **Limitations:** Not optimized for UAP detection; coverage and sensitivity thresholds not characterized for this purpose.
- **Referenced by:** Sean Kirkpatrick

#### 21. Gravitational Wave Detection Tools
- **Owner/Operator:** NASA
- **Description:** Instruments and analytical tools for detecting gravitational waves (e.g., related to LISA or other missions).
- **Relevance to UAP:** Mentioned by Sean Kirkpatrick as a NASA capability that could theoretically be applied to UAP analysis.
- **Limitations:** Not designed for UAP detection; extremely specialized; unclear practical applicability to UAP phenomenology.
- **Referenced by:** Sean Kirkpatrick

#### 22. Geomagnetic Wave Detection Tools
- **Owner/Operator:** NASA
- **Description:** Instruments for detecting and measuring geomagnetic disturbances and waves in Earth's magnetosphere.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Mentioned by Sean Kirkpatrick as a NASA capability potentially applicable to UAP analysis.
- **Limitations:** Same as gravitational wave tools — not designed for UAP detection.
- **Referenced by:** Sean Kirkpatrick

#### 23. International Space Station (ISS) High-Speed Imaging
- **Owner/Operator:** NASA
- **Description:** Cameras and imaging systems aboard the ISS, including high-speed imaging capabilities.
- **Relevance to UAP:** David Spergel referenced ISS high-speed imaging data as instrumental in discovering and studying "sprites" — upward-going lightning phenomena first reported by commercial pilots and initially disbelieved until high-speed imaging from the ISS confirmed them.
- **Limitations:** Limited temporal coverage; depends on ISS orbit and crew operations; not a continuous monitoring system.
- **Referenced by:** David Spergel

#### 24. James Webb Space Telescope (JWST)
- **Owner/Operator:** NASA
- **Description:** Space-based infrared observatory for deep-space astronomy.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Referenced by David Spergel as an example of a well-calibrated, well-characterized scientific instrument — contrasting with the uncalibrated, poorly documented data typically available for UAP analysis. Also mentioned in the context of how even well-designed instruments produce artifacts (saturation bleeding, dust reflections, ghosting) that can appear anomalous.
- **Limitations:** Not designed for Earth/atmosphere observation; pointed away from Earth; any Earth-directed use would damage detectors.
- **Referenced by:** David Spergel

#### 25. Hubble Space Telescope (HST)
- **Owner/Operator:** NASA
- **Description:** Space-based optical/UV observatory for deep-space astronomy.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Referenced by David Spergel as an example of a well-calibrated instrument, and to illustrate that even the best instruments produce confusing artifacts (ghosting, reflections, saturation effects) when used outside their design parameters.
- **Limitations:** Never pointed at or near the Sun (would destroy detectors); off-axis bright sources cause ghosting and reflections; not designed for atmospheric or terrestrial observation.
- **Referenced by:** David Spergel

#### 26. Commercial Satellite Company Data
- **Owner/Operator:** Private commercial space companies
- **Description:** Data from commercial Earth observation and imaging satellites operated by private companies.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Sean Kirkpatrick and Jennifer Buss noted that commercial space companies have "troves of data" that are well-calibrated but collected for purposes other than UAP detection. Nicola Fox noted that satellite companies are doing persistent collection.
- **Limitations:** Collected for commercial purposes, not UAP detection; may be proprietary and not openly available; single-sensor perspective; companies lobbied to present but data was not used in the study.
- **Referenced by:** Sean Kirkpatrick, Jennifer Buss, Nicola Fox

#### 27. NOAA Sensors / Data
- **Owner/Operator:** NOAA (National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration)
- **Description:** NOAA's suite of environmental monitoring satellites, ground stations, and atmospheric/oceanic sensors.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Sean Kirkpatrick recommended evaluating NOAA sensors alongside NASA and FAA sensors for UAP detection capability. Paula Bontempi noted NOAA as a potential federal partner for UAP data.
- **Limitations:** Not designed for UAP detection; focused on weather and environmental monitoring.
- **Referenced by:** Sean Kirkpatrick, Paula Bontempi

### Ground-Based Scientific Instruments

#### 28. Astrophysical Ground-Based Observatories
- **Owner/Operator:** Academia / NSF / DOE / International partners
- **Description:** Ground-based astronomical observatories designed for sky surveys, time-domain astronomy, and transient detection.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Federica Bianco recommended evaluating whether these could detect UAP-like objects. Modern observatories are designed for time-anomaly detection and tracking rapidly moving objects (NEOs, asteroids, meteors), which share some characteristics with UAP search requirements.
- **Limitations:** Pointed upward at space, not optimized for atmospheric objects; limited field of regard for terrestrial targets; not calibrated for atmospheric phenomenology.
- **Referenced by:** Federica Bianco, David Grinspoon

#### 29. Geophysical Ground-Based Observatories
- **Owner/Operator:** Academia / NSF
- **Description:** Ground-based instruments monitoring geophysical phenomena including seismic, magnetic, and atmospheric electrical activity.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Sean Kirkpatrick recommended evaluating large-scale ground-based scientific instrumentation for UAP detection, including geophysical observatories.
- **Limitations:** Not designed for aerial object detection; sensitivity to UAP signatures is unknown.
- **Referenced by:** Sean Kirkpatrick, Federica Bianco

#### 30. Fast Radio Burst (FRB) Detection Instruments
- **Owner/Operator:** Australian observatory (Parkes/MURRI) / Academia
- **Description:** Radio telescope arrays designed to detect fast radio bursts — brief, intense pulses of radio waves from space.
- **Relevance to UAP:** David Spergel used the FRB discovery story as an analogy: some bursts were real (fascinating cosmic phenomena), but some were caused by a microwave oven being opened prematurely at lunchtime. Lesson: anomalies can be either genuinely novel or mundane artifacts — careful calibration and metadata are essential.
- **Limitations:** Designed for deep-space radio signals, not atmospheric objects; the microwave artifact illustrates how even sensitive instruments can pick up local mundane sources.
- **Referenced by:** David Spergel

#### 31. Near-Earth Object (NEO) / Asteroid Detection Facilities
- **Owner/Operator:** NASA / Academia
- **Description:** Ground- and space-based facilities for detecting and tracking near-Earth objects, asteroids, and comets.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Federica Bianco and David Grinspoon noted that these facilities are designed to detect rapidly moving objects in space, which could conceptually be adapted or analyzed for UAP detection. David Spergel also mentioned the expanded UAP definition (Anomalous, not just Aerial) opens the door to solar system observations.
- **Limitations:** Designed for objects beyond Earth's atmosphere; not optimized for atmospheric targets.
- **Referenced by:** Federica Bianco, David Grinspoon, David Spergel

#### 32. Meteor Detection Facilities
- **Owner/Operator:** Academia
- **Description:** Networks of cameras and sensors for detecting and tracking meteors entering Earth's atmosphere.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Federica Bianco mentioned these as existing infrastructure that could potentially contribute to UAP detection, given their ability to track fast-moving atmospheric objects.
- **Limitations:** Optimized for high-altitude, high-speed meteors; limited to specific locations; not designed for general aerial anomaly detection.
- **Referenced by:** Federica Bianco

#### 33. UAP-Specific Detection Facilities (Under Development)
- **Owner/Operator:** Various (academic, private, government)
- **Description:** New facilities being developed specifically designed to detect UAP, complying with the characteristics described by panelists (multi-sensor, calibrated, multi-platform).
- **Relevance to UAP:** Federica Bianco noted that some efforts are underway to develop purpose-built UAP detection facilities that meet the scientific standards the panel recommends.
- **Limitations:** Still in development; not yet operational at scale.
- **Referenced by:** Federica Bianco

#### 34. Weather Service Balloon Tracking Data
- **Owner/Operator:** NOAA / National Weather Service
- **Description:** Tracking data from ~92 weather stations that release balloons twice daily (at 0000Z and 1200Z). Balloons fly up to 100,000 feet with ~2-hour duration.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Mike Freie noted that at least 184 weather balloon flights occur daily in the CONUS (not including university and hobbyist launches). These are tracked and their positions provided to ATC, serving as a known background source that must be accounted for in UAP analysis.
- **Limitations:** Well-characterized and trackable, but their large daily numbers create significant background "noise" for UAP identification.
- **Referenced by:** Mike Freie, Nadia Drake

#### 35. GPS Tracking on Commercial/Hobbyist Balloons
- **Owner/Operator:** Commercial operators / Hobbyists
- **Description:** GPS position transmitters on commercial and hobbyist balloons launched under Part 101 regulations.
- **Relevance to UAP:** These provide tracking data to ensure non-interference with air traffic. However, university and hobbyist balloons add to the background of aerial objects that must be filtered out.
- **Limitations:** Not all hobbyist balloons may be tracked or registered; adds to the difficulty of distinguishing UAP from known objects.
- **Referenced by:** Mike Freie

### Crowdsource/Public Data

#### 36. Eyewitness / Citizen Science Reports
- **Owner/Operator:** General Public
- **Description:** Civilian eyewitness accounts of UAP sightings, submitted through various channels.
- **Relevance to UAP:** The most common form of UAP data currently available. Nadia Drake, Federica Bianco, and others noted that eyewitness reports alone are insufficient for scientific analysis but may contain useful information (e.g., persistent sighting locations, seasonality patterns).
- **Limitations:** Subjective and unverifiable; lack quantitative data; affected by cognitive bias, optical illusions, and environmental factors; stigma suppresses reporting; cannot establish altitude, speed, or distance without corroborating sensor data.
- **Referenced by:** Nadia Drake, Federica Bianco, David Spergel, Sean Kirkpatrick, Nicola Fox, Jennifer Buss

#### 37. Cell Phone / Smartphone Data (Location, Speed, Imagery)
- **Owner/Operator:** General Public (consumer devices)
- **Description:** Data from smartphones including imagery, GPS location, speed, time stamps, and other sensor metadata.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Sean Kirkpatrick noted that iPhone imagery alone is "generally not helpful" for UAP analysis unless the observer is very close to the object. However, the ancillary metadata (location, speed, time) from multiple phones simultaneously could be very valuable. David Spergel advocated for a NASA-backed app to standardize crowdsourced data collection.
- **Limitations:** Consumer-grade cameras lack calibration; imagery quality is poor for distant objects; single-perspective views create parallax errors; metadata may be inconsistent across devices and platforms.
- **Referenced by:** Sean Kirkpatrick, David Spergel, Federica Bianco

#### 38. Social Media Data
- **Owner/Operator:** Public platforms (various)
- **Description:** UAP-related posts, videos, and reports shared on social media platforms.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Josh Sumeta (via audience question) asked whether social media surges could be used to "queue" sensors to areas of interest, similar to how Google search trends help track disease outbreaks. Federica Bianco noted that anomaly detection algorithms can find unknowns, not just known patterns.
- **Limitations:** Unverified content; subject to manipulation; lacks scientific metadata; temporal and geographic accuracy is unreliable.
- **Referenced by:** Josh Sumeta (audience question), Federica Bianco

### Databases/Archives

#### 39. AARO UAP Case Database (~800+ cases)
- **Owner/Operator:** All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO), Pentagon
- **Description:** AARO's database of UAP reports collected from DOD, IC, and other sources. Grew from ~650 cases at the time of Kirkpatrick's congressional hearing to over 800 at the time of this meeting. Receives 50–100 new reports per month. Includes data from DOD sensors, FAA data integration, and historical records review.
- **Relevance to UAP:** The primary government database for UAP analysis. Kirkpatrick reported that only 2–5% of cases display potentially anomalous signatures. The majority are readily explainable (balloons, drones, commercial aircraft).
- **Limitations:** Most cases lack sufficient data for resolution; heavily biased toward military operational areas and sensor locations; no space or maritime reports in current holdings; a large number of cases remain "technically unresolved" due to insufficient data rather than anomalous behavior.
- **Referenced by:** Sean Kirkpatrick, Nadia Drake

#### 40. AARO Historical UAP Records (Archival)
- **Owner/Operator:** AARO / DOD
- **Description:** The U.S. government's historical UAP records, which AARO is "meticulously reviewing" as part of its scientific plan.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Sean Kirkpatrick described this as part of AARO's comprehensive review effort. Federica Bianco recommended applying AI/ML tools to search through archived scientific data for anomalous signatures that may correlate with UAP reports.
- **Limitations:** Historical data quality varies; many records are incomplete or poorly documented; classified records limit open scientific analysis.
- **Referenced by:** Sean Kirkpatrick, Federica Bianco

#### 41. FAA Domestic Event Network / UAP Reports Archive
- **Owner/Operator:** FAA
- **Description:** Archive of UAP reports filed by air traffic controllers through the FAA's domestic event network.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Mike Freie confirmed these reports are archived. Mike Gold asked whether they feed into AARO's database; Freie presumed they do.
- **Limitations:** Very low reporting volume (3–5/month historically); controller reports, not pilot reports; archived in processed form, not raw sensor data.
- **Referenced by:** Mike Freie, Mike Gold

#### 42. NASA Open Data Portal (data.nasa.gov)
- **Owner/Operator:** NASA
- **Description:** NASA's public open data portal providing access to tens of thousands of datasets from NASA missions, freely available to the public.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Nicola Fox explicitly recommended this as a resource for examining NASA data that could be relevant to UAP analysis. Federica Bianco noted NASA's experience in curating data for AI/ML readiness could be leveraged for UAP data.

- **Referenced by:** Nicola Fox, Federica Bianco

#### 43. NASA Earth Science Data Archives
- **Owner/Operator:** NASA
- **Description:** Calibrated, rigorously quality-controlled Earth science datasets from NASA's satellite missions and ground networks, including "quick look" preliminary data and fully processed science-grade data.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Panelists discussed the potential for analyzing these archives for UAP correlative data. Nicola Fox emphasized NASA's rigorous calibration protocols — "we don't release anything until it's really perfect."
- **Limitations:** Not collected for UAP detection; sensors are tuned for specific Earth science objectives, not aerial anomaly detection; frame rates may be insufficient for fast-moving objects.

#### 44. data.gov/open/data (Federal Open Data Portal)
- **Owner/Operator:** U.S. Government

- **Relevance to UAP:** Nicola Fox recommended this as a resource for exploring the breadth of U.S. government open data that could be relevant to UAP analysis.
- **Limitations:** General portal; data quality and relevance vary by agency and dataset.

#### 45. UAP Tracking Organizations (Nonprofit/For-Profit)
- **Owner/Operator:** Various nonprofit and for-profit organizations
- **Description:** Independent organizations that track and catalog UAP sightings from civilian reports.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Jennifer Buss noted these organizations collect large volumes of sighting data. However, the panel concluded that much of this input is "not considered scientific data" — it lacks unbiased information, is not repeatable, and typically relies on eyewitness accounts.
- **Limitations:** Not scientifically validated; data is inconsistent, uncalibrated, and poorly documented; subject to reporting bias and stigma effects.
- **Referenced by:** Jennifer Buss

#### 46. Five Eyes Partner Data
- **Owner/Operator:** UK, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, United States (intelligence sharing alliance)
- **Description:** Intelligence and sensor data shared among the Five Eyes partner nations for UAP analysis.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Sean Kirkpatrick reported holding the first Five Eyes forum on UAP and establishing data-sharing relationships with these partners, who will send their data and analysis to feed into AARO's process.
- **Limitations:** Likely classified; limited to military/intelligence data; scientific community access is restricted.
- **Referenced by:** Sean Kirkpatrick, David Spergel

#### 47. SETI / Technosignature Data & Methods
- **Owner/Operator:** SETI community / NASA
- **Description:** Data and analytical methods from the Search for Extraterrestrial Intelligence and technosignature research — looking for evidence of technological activity beyond Earth.
- **Relevance to UAP:** David Grinspoon noted that the SETI community has decades of experience in methodologies for determining whether a potential technosignature is a natural phenomenon, misinterpreted terrestrial technology, or genuinely anomalous. These methods could be applied to UAP analysis.
- **Limitations:** SETI data is focused on signals from deep space, not Earth's atmosphere; technosignature research as a distinct field is relatively new and has historically faced stigma.
- **Referenced by:** David Grinspoon

#### 48. Biosignature Detection Data & Methods
- **Owner/Operator:** NASA Astrobiology / Academia
- **Description:** Data and methods for detecting biosignatures — anomalous gases, chemical signatures, or other indicators of biological activity on other planets.
- **Relevance to UAP:** David Grinspoon and Paula Bontemps discussed parallels between biosignature analysis and UAP analysis — both involve identifying anomalies, seeking more data, and ruling out mundane explanations. The astrobiology community's rigorous approach to anomaly validation could inform UAP methodology.
- **Limitations:** Focused on planetary atmospheres and extraterrestrial environments, not Earth's atmosphere; well-standardized data sets exist for biosignatures but not for UAPs.
- **Referenced by:** David Grinspoon, Paula Bontemps

#### 49. NASA Historical Records
- **Owner/Operator:** NASA
- **Description:** NASA's institutional historical archives.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Raised during public Q&A — a question was asked about whether the NASA historian had reviewed historical records for UAP references. David Spergel responded that the study's goal is creating a roadmap, not conducting a complete historical archival study, though they have been informed by some reported events.
- **Limitations:** Not systematically reviewed for UAP content as part of this study; NASA astronauts reported no formal or informal briefings about UAPs.
- **Referenced by:** Karen Fox (public question), David Spergel, Scott Kelly

## Additional Notes

### Key Cross-Cutting Themes

1. **Calibration Gap:** Across nearly every data source, panelists emphasized that existing sensors were **not designed or calibrated for UAP detection**. This was the single most-repeated limitation.

2. **Multi-Sensor Corroboration:** Josh Sumeta's analysis of the "Go Fast" video and Sean Kirkpatrick's P-3 footage analysis both demonstrated that single-sensor data is easily misinterpreted. Multi-sensor, multi-platform, multi-site observation is essential.

3. **Classified vs. Open Data:** A major theme was the tension between classified DOD/IC data (rich but inaccessible) and open civilian data (accessible but insufficiently calibrated). NASA's role was framed as bridging this gap with open, well-characterized data.

4. **Background "Noise":** The sheer volume of known aerial objects — 45,000 daily FAA flights, 880,000 registered drones, 184+ daily weather balloons, plus military equipment, balloons, and ionospheric phenomena — creates an enormous challenge for anomaly detection.

5. **Stigma and Under-Reporting:** Multiple speakers noted that stigma significantly suppresses reporting, particularly among commercial pilots, meaning the actual number of UAP events is likely higher than recorded.

6. **Frame Rate as Critical Parameter:** Shelley Wright specifically identified frame rate as a major limitation of existing NASA and other sensors for detecting fast-moving objects.

7. **AI/ML Readiness:** Federica Bianco emphasized that AI/ML methods cannot be applied until data meets FAIR standards (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable). Current UAP data does not meet these standards.
