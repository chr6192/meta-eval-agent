## 1. Government/Military Sensors

### 1.1 DOD Tactical Sensors (F-35, F/A-18, etc.)

- **Owner/Operator:** Department of Defense
- **Description:** Radar and imaging sensors aboard military fighter aircraft used to identify and track objects. Predominantly designed to identify known objects and support weapon targeting.
- **Relevance to UAP:** The vast majority of UAP reports come from DOD sensors. These are the primary source of reported UAP observations. However, they are not scientific instruments — they are designed to identify known threats and put weapons on targets, not to collect calibrated scientific data.
- **Limitations:** DOD sensors are not scientific sensors; they are not calibrated for scientific data collection. They are designed for one purpose: to identify a known object and engage it. Data from these platforms is classified not because of what is observed but because of the sensor platform's capabilities. They don't point where UAP researchers want them to point. Legal restrictions prevent pointing surveillance apparatus at domestic areas.
- **Referenced By:** Sean Kirkpatrick, David Spergel

### 1.2 MQ-9 Electro-Optical (EO) Sensor

- **Owner/Operator:** DOD (Air Force)
- **Description:** Electro-optical sensor aboard MQ-9 Reaper drone used for surveillance and targeting. Captured footage of metallic spherical orb in Middle East (2022).
- **Relevance to UAP:** The MQ-9 EO sensor captured one of the most typical UAP observations — a metallic orb. This is an example of a well-understood DOD sensor providing UAP-relevant data.
- **Limitations:** EO sensors can produce artifacts; the object was confirmed as real (not a sensor artifact), but the sensor provided limited resolution and insufficient data to reach definitive conclusions about the object's nature. No enigmatic technical capabilities or threats to air safety were observed.
- **Referenced By:** Sean Kirkpatrick, Walter

### 1.3 P-3 Aircraft Sensor Platform

- **Owner/Operator:** DOD (Navy)
- **Description:** Sensor system aboard P-3 maritime patrol aircraft on training missions. Captured video of three dots moving back and forth that were initially reported as UAP.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Demonstrated how sensor jitter and platform motion can create misperception. The three objects reported as anomalous were ultimately identified as commercial aircraft on a flight corridor heading into a major airport, confirmed via air traffic control data.
- **Limitations:** Sensor jitter creates apparent motion that can spoof both trained pilots and sensors. Unstabilized gimbals produce camera shake that makes objects appear to move erratically. Perception of the operator may not match reality.
- **Referenced By:** Sean Kirkpatrick

### 1.4 Navy Aviator Targeting Pods (ATFLIR / Infrared Imaging)

- **Owner/Operator:** DOD (Navy)
- **Description:** Ranging and infrared imaging systems on Navy fighter aircraft (F/A-18). Provide elevation angle, azimuth angle, target range, aircraft altitude, time reference, and indicated airspeed. Used in "Go Fast" video from USS Theodore Roosevelt (2015).
- **Relevance to UAP:** The Navy aviator systems provide the most detailed contextual information for UAP cases. Using multi-sensor data (ranging + infrared imaging), critical parameters like altitude and velocity can be calculated under certain assumptions. The "Go Fast" video analysis showed that apparent rapid motion was largely due to parallax from the fast-moving sensing platform, with the object actually moving at ~40 mph — consistent with wind speeds at 13,000 ft.
- **Limitations:** Parallax effects from the sensing platform can create illusion of rapid object motion. Cognitive bias from unfamiliar perspectives. Range information can have uncertainties. Single-sensor observations cannot independently assess size or speed without range data.
- **Referenced By:** Josh Sumeta, Sean Kirkpatrick

### 1.5 Intelligence Community (IC) Sensors

- **Owner/Operator:** Intelligence Community
- **Description:** Classified intelligence sensors used for surveillance and reconnaissance.
- **Relevance to UAP:** IC sensors are described as being very close to scientific sensors — calibrated, high precision, well-characterized. They could provide higher-quality UAP data than standard DOD tactical sensors.
- **Limitations:** Classified; data cannot be shared openly. Not accessible to NASA's independent study team (which uses only unclassified data).
- **Referenced By:** Sean Kirkpatrick

### 1.6 Spy Satellites

- **Owner/Operator:** Intelligence Community / DOD
- **Description:** Classified imaging satellites used for intelligence collection.
- **Relevance to UAP:** If a spy satellite takes an image of a balloon, that data is classified — not because of the subject, but to protect technical capabilities. These platforms could potentially detect anomalous objects from space.
- **Limitations:** Data is classified to protect technical capabilities from foreign nations. Cannot be used for open scientific study.
- **Referenced By:** David Spergel

### 1.7 AARO Purpose-Built Dedicated Sensors

- **Owner/Operator:** AARO (All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office) / DOD
- **Description:** Custom-designed sensors purpose-built to detect, track, and characterize UAP targets based on the typical UAP characteristics identified by AARO (size, altitude, speed, etc.). Deployed in select areas for surveillance.
- **Relevance to UAP:** These are the first sensors specifically designed for UAP detection. They use a bootstrap method — broad spectrum search across few indicators to gather initial data, then refine and narrow sensor capabilities based on findings.
- **Limitations:** Bootstrap method means initially searching across very few indicators. Need refinement over time. Cannot point at domestic areas without legal restrictions.
- **Referenced By:** Sean Kirkpatrick

### 1.8 DOD/DOE National Labs

- **Owner/Operator:** DOD and DOE
- **Description:** National laboratories (e.g., Livermore, Los Alamos) that explore current state-of-the-art fundamental physics related to UAP observations.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Used to explore fundamental physics of UAP observations — current understanding of maneuverability, speed, signature management, propulsion, and expected signatures.
- **Limitations:** Some work is classified; not discussed in detail in the open meeting.
- **Referenced By:** Sean Kirkpatrick

### 1.9 Classified DOD/DHS Systems

- **Owner/Operator:** DOD / DHS
- **Description:** Classified surveillance and detection systems operated by DOD and DHS, not detailed in the FAA presentation.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Acknowledged to exist but not discussed due to classification.
- **Limitations:** Classified; not available for civilian/open analysis.
- **Referenced By:** Mike Freie

## 2. Civilian Aviation Systems

### 2.1 FAA Short-Range Radars (Airport Surveillance Radar — ASR)

- **Owner/Operator:** FAA
- **Description:** Terminal radar systems located at high- and medium-density airports. Detection range of 40–60 miles; altitude detection up to approximately 24,000 feet. Rotate at 12 RPM for terminal surveillance.
- **Relevance to UAP:** These non-cooperative radars (RF energy transmitted, reflected off target, return received) could potentially detect UAP that do not have cooperative avionics. They form part of the national airspace surveillance infrastructure.
- **Limitations:** Designed for manned aircraft detection; detection capability depends heavily on target size. A 1-square-meter target (sphere of 1 meter diameter, comparable to F-15/F-18) is the baseline model. A small drone (~0.01 sq m) is 100x harder to detect. Filtering removes ground clutter, biologicals (insects), and dust. Not optimized for detecting small, slow, or low-altitude anomalous objects. Coverage gaps exist, especially in mountainous western U.S. and at low altitudes.
- **Referenced By:** Mike Freie

### 2.2 FAA Long-Range Radars (ARSR-4 / CRSR)

- **Owner/Operator:** FAA
- **Description:** Long-range radar systems with detection range of 200–250 nautical miles and altitude detection up to 100,000 feet. ARSR-4s located around the perimeter of the continental U.S. for homeland defense; CRSR systems on the interior. Rotate at 5 RPM.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Provide broader coverage at higher altitudes. Could detect anomalous objects at higher altitudes across wider areas. Also serve a national defense and homeland security mission.
- **Limitations:** Same target-size dependency as short-range radars. Line-of-sight limitations from curvature of the earth and terrain screening (especially mountains in western U.S.). Not designed for UAP detection. Operated in constant search mode (fixed face, rotating at fixed RPM) — cannot be tasked.
- **Referenced By:** Mike Freie

### 2.3 ADS-B (Automatic Dependent Surveillance-Broadcast)

- **Owner/Operator:** FAA
- **Description:** Cooperative surveillance system where aircraft transmit their GPS position, altitude, and identity via broadcast. Serves as a primary and preferred data source for airspace management. Good coverage across U.S. down to approximately 1,500 feet above ground level.
- **Relevance to UAP:** ADS-B provides excellent coverage for cooperative (known, transmitting) aircraft. It is the preferred data source for ATC. However, by definition, ADS-B data is neither unknown nor anomalous — any aircraft transmitting ADS-B is identified.
- **Limitations:** Cooperative system only — does not detect non-transmitting objects. Completely irrelevant for detecting anomalous/unknown objects that lack ADS-B avionics. Not considered part of non-cooperative surveillance capability.
- **Referenced By:** Mike Freie

### 2.4 FAA ERAM/STAR Display Systems

- **Owner/Operator:** FAA
- **Description:** En Route Automation Modernization (ERAM) and Standard Terminal Automation Replacement System (STAR) — processed radar display systems used by controllers at en-route centers and TRACONs.
- **Relevance to UAP:** The data recorded and archived is the processed form displayed on these systems, not raw radar data. This processed data merges cooperative and non-cooperative targets.
- **Limitations:** Data retained is processed (filtered), not raw IQ data. Raw IQ data would be huge (gigabytes, massive volumes) and cost-prohibitive to collect. Collecting raw data would require modifying systems or introducing new collection methods. Data is retained for a period measured in months for legal requirements.
- **Referenced By:** Mike Freie, Walter

### 2.5 FAA Domestic Event Network (DEN)

- **Owner/Operator:** FAA
- **Description:** An FAA organization/function that receives UAP sighting reports from air traffic controllers. Reports are archived.
- **Relevance to UAP:** The primary mechanism for ATC UAP reporting. Provides a structured process defined in the air traffic controller order. Reports are presumably shared with AARO's database.
- **Limitations:** Very low reporting rate — historically 3–5 reports per month across all 14,000 controllers. Process is defined for controllers, but no known equivalent process for pilots. Significant uptick occurred after Chinese balloon incident (68 reports in February), suggesting event-driven reporting rather than systematic collection.
- **Referenced By:** Mike Freie, Mike Gold

### 2.6 FAA Radar Data Archive

- **Owner/Operator:** FAA
- **Description:** Archive of processed radar data retained for legal requirements (investigation of accidents/incidents).
- **Relevance to UAP:** Contains historical processed radar data that could potentially be searched for anomalous radar returns. The data is what was displayed on ERAM/STAR systems.
- **Limitations:** Data is stored in processed form, not raw. Retention period is measured in months, not years. Raw IQ data is not retained — would be cost-prohibitive. Collecting raw data retroactively would require system modifications.
- **Referenced By:** Walter, Mike Freie

### 2.7 FAA Air Traffic Controller UAP Reporting Process

- **Owner/Operator:** FAA
- **Description:** A procedure defined in the air traffic controller order allowing controllers to report sightings of unknown or anomalous objects.
- **Relevance to UAP:** The formal civilian mechanism for UAP reporting in national airspace. However, reporting rates are extremely low.
- **Limitations:** Stigma may impair reporting. Only addresses controller reports — no established pilot-facing reporting process was described. Very small percentage of total operations result in reports.
- **Referenced By:** Mike Freie, Federica Bianco

### 2.8 National Weather Service Balloon Tracking System

- **Owner/Operator:** National Weather Service (NWS)
- **Description:** Tracking system where 92 NWS stations coordinate with local ATC facilities before releasing weather balloons, providing tracking data throughout flights. Balloons are released twice daily (0600 and 1200 Zulu), typically with 2-hour duration, ascending to 100,000 feet before burst and payload descent.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Weather balloons are a major source of background clutter that can be mistaken for UAP. 184 balloon flights daily in the U.S. alone; worldwide ~1,600 launches per day. Understanding balloon tracking data is essential for filtering out mundane objects.
- **Limitations:** Does not include university or hobbyist balloons, or private company balloons, which are typically small. Commercial balloons transmit GPS position.
- **Referenced By:** Mike Freie, Nadia Drake

### 2.9 FAA Drone Registry (Part 107)

- **Owner/Operator:** FAA
- **Description:** Registry of 880,000 registered small drones (Part 107) in the U.S., plus 1.69 million recreational/model small UAS. Many tens of thousands operated daily by commercial operators.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Drones represent a significant background challenge for UAP identification. They are regulated to operate below 400 feet and avoid manned aircraft, but private drone operations are not tracked or scheduled by ATC.
- **Limitations:** Unclear how many private drone operators fly unsanctioned flights. Drones are not controlled by ATC and not on scheduled flight plans. Their small size (~0.01 sq m radar cross-section) makes them difficult to detect on radar.
- **Referenced By:** Mike Freie, Nadia Drake

## 3. Space-Based Assets

### 3.1 NASA Earth Sensing Satellites

- **Owner/Operator:** NASA
- **Description:** Satellites used for Earth science observations — measuring atmospheric, oceanic, and terrestrial phenomena from the unique vantage point of space. Part of NASA's 60+ years of experience measuring phenomena in air and space.
- **Relevance to UAP:** NASA has access to earth sensing satellites that could potentially detect anomalous objects or environmental conditions associated with UAP events. AARO wants to evaluate whether any of these satellites can detect the typical UAP target package.
- **Limitations:** Not designed or calibrated for identifying anomalous phenomena. Data is collected for Earth science purposes, creating bias in what is captured. Frame rate and spatial resolution may be insufficient for detecting small, fast-moving UAP. Data can be hard to access despite being publicly available.
- **Referenced By:** Sean Kirkpatrick, Paula Bontempi, Shelley Wright

### 3.2 Hubble Space Telescope

- **Owner/Operator:** NASA
- **Description:** Space-based optical telescope designed for astronomical observations, primarily at night.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Used as an example of well-characterized, calibrated scientific instruments. Also demonstrates optical artifacts — bright stars cause detector saturation and "bleeding," while off-axis bright objects create "ghosting" from reflections off dust in optics, producing strange diffuse images.
- **Limitations:** Designed for nighttime observations. Pointing at the sun would destroy its detectors. Ghosting and saturation artifacts can produce images that appear anomalous but are actually optical effects. Data is well-calibrated but collected for astronomical purposes.
- **Referenced By:** David Spergel

### 3.3 James Webb Space Telescope

- **Owner/Operator:** NASA
- **Description:** Space-based infrared telescope for deep astronomical observations.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Referenced as another example of well-characterized NASA instruments where apparently strange phenomena (detector saturation, bleeding, ghosting from dust) can appear anomalous but are understood optical/detector effects.
- **Limitations:** Same as Hubble — well-calibrated but not designed for UAP detection. Detector artifacts from bright sources can produce apparently anomalous imagery.
- **Referenced By:** David Spergel

### 3.4 NOAA Satellites

- **Owner/Operator:** NOAA
- **Description:** Earth and environmental sensing satellites operated by the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Identified as potential sensor systems that could be evaluated for UAP detection capability, alongside NASA satellites. NOAA collects environmental data that may correlate with UAP reports.
- **Limitations:** Not designed for UAP detection. Need evaluation to determine if they could detect the UAP target package.
- **Referenced By:** Sean Kirkpatrick, Paula Bontempi

### 3.5 International Space Station (ISS) Imaging

- **Owner/Operator:** NASA
- **Description:** Imaging systems aboard the ISS, including high-speed cameras used for atmospheric and ionospheric observation.
- **Relevance to UAP:** ISS high-speed imaging was critical for confirming sprites (upward-going lightning) — an ionospheric phenomenon initially reported by commercial pilots and not believed. The ISS vantage point provides unique atmospheric observation capability. Also noted for the optical illusion example where the ISS appeared as debris in the Space Shuttle payload bay from 80 miles away.
- **Limitations:** Not designed for UAP surveillance. Limited to whatever passes through its orbital path.
- **Referenced By:** David Spergel, Scott Kelly

### 3.6 Space Shuttle Cameras

- **Owner/Operator:** NASA
- **Description:** Cameras aboard the Space Shuttle used for payload bay inspection and Earth observation.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Demonstrated extreme optical illusion — during STS-124 (2008), an object seen in the payload bay that appeared to be a bolt or tool was actually the International Space Station 80 miles away when the image was enlarged.
- **Limitations:** Space environment is extremely conducive to optical illusions; distance and perspective are difficult to judge without additional data.
- **Referenced By:** Scott Kelly

### 3.7 Commercial Satellite Data

- **Owner/Operator:** Commercial space companies
- **Description:** Private satellite companies that collect persistent Earth observation data for various commercial purposes.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Commercial companies have troves of data collected persistently. While well-calibrated, the data is collected for commercial purposes, not UAP detection. Could serve as one source among many.
- **Limitations:** Only one source of many; collected for purposes other than UAP. Well calibrated but not optimized for anomalous detection. Access may require commercial agreements.
- **Referenced By:** Jennifer Buss, Nicola Fox

### 3.8 NASA TV Live Feeds

- **Owner/Operator:** NASA
- **Description:** Real-time footage from NASA missions broadcast publicly via NASA TV.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Public questions asked whether NASA TV feeds had been cut to hide UAP sightings.
- **Limitations:** Feeds can be interrupted due to the complexity of space operations, but NASA stated they have never intentionally cut a feed to hide anything.
- **Referenced By:** Dan Evans, public questions

## 4. Ground-Based Scientific Instruments

### 4.1 Australian Radio Observatory (Parkes/Murriyang)

- **Owner/Operator:** CSIRO (Australia)
- **Description:** Sensitive radio telescope used for astronomical observations, including the discovery of Fast Radio Bursts (FRBs).
- **Relevance to UAP:** Served as an illustrative example of how large-scale scientific instruments not designed for a specific phenomenon can still detect anomalous signals. The observatory detected real FRBs (powerful cosmological explosions) but also false signals from microwave ovens opened before the timer stopped — demonstrating that even highly sensitive instruments can be spoofed by mundane sources.
- **Limitations:** Instruments sensitive but not optimized for specific phenomena. Can pick up local interference (microwave oven bursts). Need optimized software and instrumentation to search for specific signals.
- **Referenced By:** David Spergel

### 4.2 Large-Scale Ground-Based Scientific Instrumentation

- **Owner/Operator:** Various scientific institutions worldwide
- **Description:** Ground-based scientific instruments (radio telescopes, particle detectors, seismographs, etc.) that collect calibrated data continuously.
- **Relevance to UAP:** AARO recommended evaluating how existing large-scale scientific instruments could be used for UAP detection. The FRB analogy shows instruments not designed for a phenomenon can still detect it. Understanding whether any of these instruments have a chance of detecting the UAP target package (through modeling and simulation) could identify new detection opportunities.
- **Limitations:** Not designed for UAP detection. Need evaluation through modeling/simulation to determine detection potential. Would serve as "tip and cue" for other sensors rather than primary detection.
- **Referenced By:** Sean Kirkpatrick

### 4.3 Astrophysical Observatories (Ground-Based)

- **Owner/Operator:** Various academic/institutional operators
- **Description:** Ground-based astronomical observatories, including some specifically designed for detection of time-domain anomalies (transient events) and rapid-moving objects (near-Earth objects, asteroids, meteors).
- **Relevance to UAP:** Modern observatories designed for anomaly detection in the time domain and rapid-moving objects could be leveraged for UAP studies. These are already optimized for detecting unusual signals in noisy data rich with known phenomena.
- **Limitations:** Primarily designed for astronomical observation (pointing upward/at night). May not have the frame rate or spatial resolution needed for atmospheric UAP detection.
- **Referenced By:** Federica Bianco

### 4.4 Geographical/Earth Science Observatories

- **Owner/Operator:** Various institutions
- **Description:** Ground-based networks monitoring Earth's atmospheric, geological, and environmental processes.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Could provide data on environmental conditions around the time of reported UAP events, helping establish baselines of "normal" phenomena.
- **Limitations:** Not designed for UAP detection; monitoring different phenomenologies.
- **Referenced By:** Federica Bianco, Paula Bontempi

### 4.5 AARO Ground-Based Point Detectors (Proposed)

- **Owner/Operator:** AARO
- **Description:** Proposed upward-pointing ground-based detection systems for 24/7 collection monitoring in hotspot areas for three-month periods to establish "normal" baselines and detect anomalies.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Would provide continuous monitoring in areas where UAP reports cluster. Essential for pattern-of-life analysis — establishing what is normal so that what is not normal can be identified. Also addresses the legal restriction against pointing military surveillance systems at domestic areas.
- **Limitations:** Need deployment in select areas. Must operate 24/7 for at least three months per location. Coverage limited to specific deployment areas.
- **Referenced By:** Sean Kirkpatrick

### 4.6 Radiological Sensors

- **Owner/Operator:** NASA
- **Description:** Sensors for detecting radiological signatures/phenomena.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Identified as NASA capabilities that could assist AARO in UAP efforts. Could detect anomalous radiological signatures associated with UAP events.
- **Limitations:** Not designed for UAP detection. Need evaluation for UAP target relevance.
- **Referenced By:** Sean Kirkpatrick

### 4.7 Gravitational Wave Detection Tools

- **Owner/Operator:** NASA
- **Description:** Instruments and methods for detecting gravitational waves.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Identified as NASA capabilities potentially relevant to UAP detection — could detect anomalous gravitational signatures.
- **Limitations:** Not designed for atmospheric/aerial anomaly detection. Current gravitational wave detectors are designed for cosmological-scale events.
- **Referenced By:** Sean Kirkpatrick

### 4.8 Geomagnetic Wave Detection Tools

- **Owner/Operator:** NASA
- **Description:** Instruments for detecting geomagnetic variations and waves.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Identified as NASA capabilities that could assist UAP detection. Could detect anomalous geomagnetic signatures.
- **Limitations:** Need evaluation for relevance to UAP target signatures.
- **Referenced By:** Sean Kirkpatrick

## 5. Crowdsource/Public Data

### 5.1 Smartphone Data (iPhone etc.)

- **Owner/Operator:** Public (various)
- **Description:** Camera imagery and metadata (location, speed, other phenomenologies) captured by smartphones.
- **Relevance to UAP:** While imagery from a smartphone is generally not helpful unless the user is very close to the object, the ancillary metadata (location, speed, timestamps, orientation) can be very helpful when more than one phone captures data simultaneously. Could provide multi-point corroboration.
- **Limitations:** Imagery generally not helpful for distant objects. Metadata alone is limited. No standardized collection platform exists. Unstructured and uncalibrated. Needs a platform to collect, transmit, and curate both data and metadata.
- **Referenced By:** Sean Kirkpatrick

### 5.2 Eyewitness Reports

- **Owner/Operator:** Public (commercial pilots, military aviators, citizens)
- **Description:** Human observations and verbal/written descriptions of anomalous events.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Eyewitness reports are currently the primary source of UAP events. They can contain important information (persistent sighting locations, seasonality) but cannot ascertain the nature of UAP on their own. Must be joined with quantitative sensor data and biophysical/psychophysical assessments.
- **Limitations:** Often muddled and insufficient for conclusive evidence. Subject to optical illusions, parallax, and cognitive bias. Stigma discourages reporting (especially among commercial pilots). Cannot provide definitive physical parameters (size, speed, altitude). Lack quality control and data curation.
- **Referenced By:** David Spergel, Nadia Drake, Nicola Fox, Federica Bianco, Karlin Toner, Sean Kirkpatrick

### 5.3 Social Media Data (Tip & Cue)

- **Owner/Operator:** Public
- **Description:** Social media posts and trends that could indicate UAP sighting events, analogous to how Google search trends are used to track disease outbreaks.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Could serve as a real-time tip-and-cue mechanism — detecting clusters of reports that indicate something anomalous is happening in a location, enabling sensors to be pointed there.
- **Limitations:** Not yet applied to UAP. Would require real-time processing capability and coordination with sensor networks. Data is unstructured, uncalibrated, and noisy.
- **Referenced By:** Reggie Brothers

### 5.4 Crowdsourcing Platform (Proposed)

- **Owner/Operator:** NASA (proposed)
- **Description:** A proposed centralized platform for organized public UAP data collection, supporting collection and transmission of data and metadata from multiple citizen scientists.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Would enable coordinated crowdsourcing where sightings by one person trigger broader community sensor pointing. Would centralize and curate collected data. NASA's experience in data curation and coordination across disciplines and countries could support platform development.
- **Limitations:** Does not currently exist. Requires development. Must reach a large enough community to have meaningful crowd size. Needs coordination for follow-up observations. Must support FAIR standards (findability, accessibility, interoperability, reusability).
- **Referenced By:** Federica Bianco, Walter

### 5.5 Commercial Pilot Reports

- **Owner/Operator:** Commercial aviation pilots
- **Description:** Anecdotal reports of anomalous sightings from commercial airline pilots.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Commercial pilots are trained observers who spend extensive time in the airspace. They have historically reported unusual phenomena (sprites were first reported by commercial pilots). However, they are very reluctant to report anomalies due to stigma.
- **Limitations:** Stigma is a major barrier — commercial pilots are reluctant to report. No clear pilot-facing reporting process was described. Reports alone lack quantitative sensor corroboration.
- **Referenced By:** David Spergel, Karlin Toner

### 5.6 Air Traffic Controller UAP Reports

- **Owner/Operator:** FAA controllers
- **Description:** Reports from FAA air traffic controllers of anomalous objects observed on radar displays or visually.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Controllers have the combined benefit of radar data and visual observation. Their reports are formally captured through the DEN process.
- **Limitations:** Historically only 3–5 reports per month across 14,000 controllers and 45,000 daily operations. Stigma may impair reporting. Process is defined only for controllers, not for pilots.
- **Referenced By:** Mike Freie

## 6. Databases/Archives

### 6.1 AARO Database (800+ Cases)

- **Owner/Operator:** AARO (All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office) / DOD
- **Description:** The central UAP case database maintained by AARO, containing over 800 cases at the time of the meeting, growing at 50–100 new reports per month. Includes both classified and unclassified data. Covers approximately 27 years of reports.
- **Relevance to UAP:** The primary government UAP database. Contains analytic trends, UAP morphologies, heat maps of reporting areas, and typical UAP characteristics. Only 2–5% of cases display signatures that could reasonably be described as anomalous. A large number remain technically unresolved primarily due to lack of data.
- **Limitations:** Majority of cases demonstrate mundane characteristics. Large number unresolved due to insufficient data. Collection is biased — reports cluster where military/IC sensors and operators are located (not where civilian population is). Contains classified component not accessible to open study. Annual unclassified report due August 1.
- **Referenced By:** Sean Kirkpatrick, Nadia Drake

### 6.2 NASA Open Data Portal (data.nasa.gov)

- **Owner/Operator:** NASA
- **Description:** Public portal containing tens of thousands of NASA datasets, free and fully accessible. Includes Earth science, astronomical, and mission data.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Contains vast archives of NASA satellite and mission data that could potentially contain information relevant to UAP events or environmental conditions around reported UAP sightings. All data goes through rigorous calibration before release. Quick Look data available for preliminary findings.
- **Limitations:** Data was not collected for UAP identification, creating collection bias. Hard to access despite being publicly available. Sensors were not calibrated for identifying anomalous phenomena. Requires significant effort to retrieve and analyze for UAP relevance.
- **Referenced By:** Nicola Fox

### 6.3 data.gov / Open Data Portal

- **Owner/Operator:** US Government
- **Description:** Federal open data portal providing overview of where to find archives for US government science and mission data sites.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Provides access point to various federal datasets beyond NASA, including those from NOAA, FAA, Commerce, and other agencies that could contain UAP-relevant data.
- **Limitations:** Data collected for purposes other than UAP identification. Same limitations regarding bias, calibration, and accessibility as NASA data.
- **Referenced By:** Nicola Fox

### 6.4 FAA UAP/NFA Reporting Archive

- **Owner/Operator:** FAA
- **Description:** Archive of UAP and Non-Flight Activity (NFA) reports submitted through the Domestic Event Network by air traffic controllers.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Contains historical UAP sighting reports from controllers. Presumably feeds into AARO's database. Significant uptick in reporting after Chinese balloon incident in February (68 reports).
- **Limitations:** Very low baseline reporting rate (3–5/month). Reports may be incomplete. Unclear how long data is retained. No clear pilot-facing reporting process.
- **Referenced By:** Mike Freie, Mike Gold

### 6.5 NASA Partner Archival Data (ML-Ready)

- **Owner/Operator:** NASA
- **Description:** NASA and NASA-partner archival data that has been recently prepared for machine learning and AI applications — data served to the community ready for AI methods.
- **Relevance to UAP:** NASA has spearheaded an effort to review and prepare archival data for machine learning/AI, making it directly usable for automated analysis. This experience and infrastructure could be leveraged for UAP data curation.
- **Limitations:** Current status of UAP data makes this lift much harder than for astrophysical data. UAP data doesn't meet FAIR standards (findability, accessibility, interoperability, reusability). Data is inconsistent, inhomogeneous, uncalibrated, poorly documented, and incomplete.
- **Referenced By:** Federica Bianco

### 6.6 Archived Scientific Data (Global)

- **Owner/Operator:** Various scientific institutions worldwide
- **Description:** Calibrated large-scale scientific data from ground-based instrumentations around the world — radio telescopes, particle detectors, seismographic networks, etc.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Could be searched using AI/ML tools for anomalous signatures that may correlate with UAP reports. Represents a wealth of calibrated data not originally collected for UAP purposes.
- **Limitations:** Not designed for UAP detection. Requires AI/ML tools to search. Correlation with UAP reports is speculative. Need to determine which datasets have detection potential.
- **Referenced By:** Sean Kirkpatrick

### 6.7 OODI/UAP Task Force Records

- **Owner/Operator:** DOD
- **Description:** Previous UAP reporting data from the Office of the Director of National Intelligence (ODNI) UAP report and the UAP Task Force, predecessor to AARO. Approximately 500 cases initially reported.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Historical UAP records that have been transitioned into AARO's database. Reports have grown from ~500 to ~800+ during the panel's tenure.
- **Limitations:** Historical; data quality varies. Originally limited to military/IC reporting sources.
- **Referenced By:** Karlin Toner

### 6.8 SETI Radio/Optical Data Archives

- **Owner/Operator:** SETI community / various institutions
- **Description:** Long-standing archives of radio and optical observations collected for the Search for Extraterrestrial Intelligence. Well-standardized datasets covering decades.
- **Relevance to UAP:** The SETI community has relevant expertise in methodologies for determining whether observations are real signals, known/unknown natural phenomena, or misinterpreted terrestrial technology. Data analysis approaches (anomaly detection, signal verification) are directly applicable to UAP studies.
- **Limitations:** Focused on distant signals, not atmospheric phenomena. Classical SETI data is well standardized but the newer "technosignature" approach has less accumulated data. Stigma around technosignatures has historically limited NASA investment.
- **Referenced By:** David Grinspoon

### 6.9 Biosignature/Technosignature Datasets

- **Owner/Operator:** NASA astrobiology community
- **Description:** Standardized datasets collected for identifying biosignatures (anomalous gases in planetary atmospheres, etc.) and technosignatures (evidence of technological activity).
- **Relevance to UAP:** Well-standardized data with mature analysis techniques for identifying anomalous signals. The methodology for distinguishing natural vs. technological vs. unknown signals is directly applicable to UAP classification. Lessons from data standardization should be applied to UAP field.
- **Limitations:** Focused on planetary/atmospheric signatures, not aerial objects. Technosignature data is relatively new and limited compared to biosignature data.
- **Referenced By:** David Grinspoon, Anamaria Berea

### 6.10 Five Eyes Data Sharing Forum

- **Owner/Operator:** Five Eyes alliance (US, UK, Canada, Australia, New Zealand)
- **Description:** Newly established intelligence data sharing forum on UAP, including reporting, analysis, calibration, and data sharing protocols between partner nations.
- **Relevance to UAP:** International partnership expanding UAP data beyond US-only sources. Establishing protocols for data sharing, reporting methods, and calibration across allied nations. Partner nations will send information and data to AARO's process.
- **Limitations:** Newly established; protocols still being defined. Limited to Five Eyes nations currently. Classified/intelligence-focused, not openly accessible.
- **Referenced By:** Sean Kirkpatrick

### 6.11 FAA Air Traffic Control Data (Corroboration)

- **Owner/Operator:** FAA
- **Description:** Air traffic control flight tracking data used to corroborate or resolve UAP sightings. In the P-3 case, ATC data was used to match three "UAP" objects to aircraft on a known flight corridor heading into a major airport for landing.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Essential for resolving UAP cases — allows cross-referencing of unknown radar/visual detections against known flight plans and ADS-B data. Critical for eliminating mundane sources.
- **Limitations:** Only covers cooperative/known flights. Does not track unscheduled or non-transmitting objects. Requires integration with AARO's analysis process (FAA data recently integrated, causing jump in AARO cases).
- **Referenced By:** Sean Kirkpatrick

### 6.12 NASA Historical Records

- **Owner/Operator:** NASA
- **Description:** NASA's historical archives of missions, observations, and records spanning 60+ years.
- **Relevance to UAP:** A public question asked whether the NASA historian should go through historical records for UAP evidence. Scott Kelly confirmed that in his 20 years at NASA, no formal or informal discussions about UAPs or UFOs had occurred.
- **Limitations:** No systematic review of historical records for UAP-relevant data has been conducted. Not organized for UAP search.
- **Referenced By:** Public questions, Scott Kelly

### 6.13 National Weather Service Balloon Launch Data

- **Owner/Operator:** NWS
- **Description:** Scheduled balloon launch data from 92 stations, twice daily, with tracking and coordination with ATC. 184 flights/day in the U.S.; approximately 1,600 worldwide daily.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Critical background data for distinguishing weather balloons from anomalous objects. Understanding balloon launch schedules, trajectories, and characteristics is essential for filtering mundane sources.
- **Limitations:** Does not include university, hobbyist, or private company balloons. Commercial balloons transmit GPS position but weather balloons have different tracking.
- **Referenced By:** Mike Freie, Nadia Drake

## Notes on Cross-Cutting Themes

1. **Data Quality Crisis:** Nearly every speaker emphasized that the fundamental problem is lack of high-quality, well-calibrated data. Current UAP data is "unsystematic and fragmented across various agencies, often using instruments uncalibrated for scientific data collection" (David Spergel).

2. **Classification vs. Openness:** DOD/IC data is often classified because of the *sensor platform*, not the observed phenomenon. This creates a fundamental barrier to open scientific study. NASA's role is explicitly to work with unclassified, open data.

3. **Sensor Calibration:** DOD sensors are designed for military purposes (identifying known threats), not scientific measurement. IC sensors are closer to scientific standards (calibrated, high precision) but are classified. FAA sensors are designed for manned aircraft safety, not anomaly detection.

4. **Collection Bias:** UAP reporting clusters where military/IC sensors and operators are located — creating a geographic and operational bias. Civilian areas with different sensor coverage are underrepresented.

5. **Background Characterization:** Multiple speakers emphasized that characterizing "normal" (known objects, weather balloons, drones, commercial aircraft) is essential before anomalies can be detected. Pattern-of-life analysis and baselining were recommended.

6. **Multi-Sensor Approach:** Josh Sumeta's "Go Fast" analysis and the P-3 case both demonstrated that single-sensor observations are highly susceptible to misperception. Multi-sensor, multi-platform, multi-site data collection was recommended as essential for credible UAP analysis.

7. **Stigma and Reporting:** Commercial pilots are reluctant to report anomalies. ATC reporting is minimal (3–5/month). DOD mandated reporting is increasing cases. NASA could help destigmatize through its brand and open scientific approach.

8. **FAIR Standards:** Federica Bianco emphasized that current UAP data does not meet FAIR standards (Findability, Accessibility, Interoperability, Reusability), preventing application of AI/ML methods.

9. **Tip and Cue:** Both AARO and panel members discussed the concept of using one detection source (pilot report, social media, citizen sighting) to "tip and cue" other sensors to point at the same area — a coordinated detection strategy.
