## 1. Government / Military Sensors

### 1.1 DOD Military Sensors (General)

- **Name/Type:** Department of Defense sensor platforms (radar, EO/IR, targeting systems)
- **Owner/Operator:** U.S. Department of Defense
- **Description:** A broad category of sensors deployed across military platforms—including aircraft, ships, and ground stations—designed primarily for target identification and weapons delivery.
- **Relevance to UAP:** These sensors constitute the vast majority of UAP reports in AARO's holdings. Kirkpatrick emphasized they are the primary source of current UAP data.
- **Limitations:** Kirkpatrick stated explicitly: *"DOD sensors are not scientific sensors… DOD sensors have one purpose. They are to identify an object that is known and put a weapon on it."* They are not calibrated for scientific data collection and lack the precision needed for rigorous characterization of unknown objects. Their collection areas are biased toward national security zones, creating geographic gaps.
- **Who Referenced It:** Sean Kirkpatrick (AARO Director), David Spergel

### 1.2 Intelligence Community (IC) Sensors

- **Name/Type:** Classified intelligence-collection sensor platforms (SIGINT, IMINT, MASINT)
- **Owner/Operator:** U.S. Intelligence Community (CIA, NSA, NRO, NGA, etc.)
- **Description:** High-precision, well-calibrated sensors used for intelligence gathering.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Kirkpatrick noted that IC sensors are much closer to scientific-grade instruments than DOD sensors. They provide the highest-quality data for characterizing objects.
- **Limitations:** Data is highly classified, making it inaccessible for open scientific study. Kirkpatrick noted the tension: *"Intelligence community sensors are very close to scientific sensors. They are calibrated, they are high precision, they are everything you'd ever want to know about a thing."* But the data cannot be shared publicly.
- **Who Referenced It:** Sean Kirkpatrick

### 1.3 F-35 Fighter Jet Sensors

- **Name/Type:** Multi-spectral sensor suite on the F-35 Lightning II (EO/IR, radar, EW)
- **Owner/Operator:** U.S. Department of Defense (USAF, USMC, USN)
- **Description:** Advanced integrated sensor suite combining electro-optical targeting, infrared, radar, and electronic warfare systems.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Spergel used the F-35 as a canonical example of why military sensor data is classified—not because of what is photographed but because of the sensor capabilities revealed. Kirkpatrick proposed using F-35s to fly against weather balloons at various conditions to calibrate DOD sensors against known objects.
- **Limitations:** All imagery/data classified due to sensor capabilities. Not calibrated for scientific observation.
- **Who Referenced It:** David Spergel, Sean Kirkpatrick

### 1.4 MQ-9 Electro-Optical (EO) Sensor

- **Name/Type:** Electro-optical sensor on the MQ-9 Reaper unmanned aerial vehicle
- **Owner/Operator:** U.S. Department of Defense
- **Description:** Forward-looking infrared/electro-optical camera system mounted on the MQ-9 drone platform.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Kirkpatrick presented declassified footage of a metallic spherical orb in the Middle East (2022) captured by this sensor—the most common morphology in AARO's holdings. Walter Scott specifically asked about sensor and data-processing artifacts for this footage; Kirkpatrick confirmed the sensor characteristics are well understood and the object is real.
- **Limitations:** Single-sensor observation limits ability to determine range, absolute size, and speed. Gimbal-mounted sensor introduces jitter artifacts.
- **Who Referenced It:** Sean Kirkpatrick, Walter Scott

### 1.5 P-3 Orion Infrared/EO Sensor

- **Name/Type:** Infrared/electro-optical sensor on the P-3 Orion maritime patrol aircraft
- **Owner/Operator:** U.S. Navy
- **Description:** Infrared imaging sensor used on the P-3 aircraft during training missions.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Kirkpatrick presented newly declassified footage from a P-3 on a training mission over the western U.S. showing three dots moving back and forth—later identified as commercial aircraft on a major flight corridor. This case illustrated parallax and sensor-jitter effects that can create false impressions of anomalous motion.
- **Limitations:** Sensor defocusing, platform motion, and lack of range data created a misperception. Objects appeared much closer than they actually were (30–40 miles away).
- **Who Referenced It:** Sean Kirkpatrick

### 1.6 Navy ATFLIR / Infrared Imaging Systems

- **Name/Type:** Advanced Targeting Forward-Looking Infrared (ATFLIR) pod and related IR imaging systems on Navy fighter aircraft
- **Owner/Operator:** U.S. Navy
- **Description:** Infrared targeting pods providing ranging and thermal imaging data from naval fighter aircraft.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Josh (panel member) used Navy aviator recordings from the USS Theodore Roosevelt (2015)—the "Go Fast" video—to demonstrate how multi-sensor data (elevation angle, azimuth, target range, aircraft altitude, airspeed, time) enables reconstruction of an object's altitude and velocity. Analysis showed the object was at ~13,000 ft moving at ~40 mph—consistent with wind speed, not a fast object over the ocean.
- **Limitations:** Range data can have uncertainties. Single-perspective observations are susceptible to parallax and cognitive biases. The sensing platform's own motion (~430 mph) creates apparent motion artifacts.
- **Who Referenced It:** Josh (panelist), Sean Kirkpatrick

### 1.7 AARO Purpose-Built Dedicated Sensors

- **Name/Type:** Custom-designed sensors built specifically for UAP detection, tracking, and characterization
- **Owner/Operator:** AARO (All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office)
- **Description:** Purpose-built sensors designed to search across specific parameters matching AARO's "typical UAP target" profile (size, speed, altitude, signature characteristics).
- **Relevance to UAP:** Kirkpatrick described these as a "bootstrap method"—broad-spectrum search across a few indicators to refine understanding, then narrow sensors for targeted collection. These are being deployed in select areas for surveillance.
- **Limitations:** Still in early stages; Kirkpatrick acknowledged this is an iterative process and that the initial broad search must be refined over time.
- **Who Referenced It:** Sean Kirkpatrick

### 1.8 Ground-Based Point Detectors

- **Name/Type:** Ground-based upward-looking detection instruments
- **Owner/Operator:** AARO / DOD / Academic partners
- **Description:** Fixed ground installations designed to monitor airspace from below, pointing upward to detect objects.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Kirkpatrick identified these as necessary supplements because existing DOD/IC collection apparatus cannot legally be pointed at domestic U.S. locations without restrictions. Ground-based detectors could fill gaps in the continental U.S.
- **Limitations:** Legal restrictions on domestic surveillance limit where collection can occur. Kirkpatrick noted: *"Most people, including the government, don't like it when I point our entire collection apparatus to your backyard."*
- **Who Referenced It:** Sean Kirkpatrick

### 1.9 Five Eyes Partner Sensors

- **Name/Type:** Military and intelligence sensor platforms of Five Eyes alliance nations
- **Owner/Operator:** United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, United States
- **Description:** Shared and complementary sensor capabilities across the Five Eyes intelligence-sharing alliance.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Kirkpatrick held the first Five Eyes forum on UAPs the week before the meeting. Partners are sharing data, analysis methods, and calibration capabilities to expand the global picture.
- **Limitations:** Data sharing agreements are still being established. Bandwidth and time constraints limit the scope of initial partnerships.
- **Who Referenced It:** Sean Kirkpatrick

## 2. Civilian Aviation Systems

### 2.1 FAA Short-Range Radars (ASR)

- **Name/Type:** Airport Surveillance Radar (ASR)
- **Owner/Operator:** Federal Aviation Administration
- **Description:** Short-range radar systems located at high- and medium-density airports. Detection range: 40–60 nautical miles; altitude detection up to ~24,000 feet.
- **Relevance to UAP:** These provide the primary non-cooperative surveillance capability at airports. They detect targets based on radar cross-section (assumes 1 sq. meter target in models). Kirkpatrick integrated FAA data into AARO's holdings, which added ~100 new cases.
- **Limitations:** Coverage concentrated in the eastern U.S. due to higher population density. Western U.S. has significant gaps due to mountainous terrain screening. Cannot detect very small targets (e.g., small UAS at ~0.01 sq. meters). Filtering removes biological clutter (birds, insects, dust) that may also filter slow-moving or small UAP.
- **Who Referenced It:** Mike Freie (FAA), Sean Kirkpatrick

### 2.2 FAA Long-Range Radars (ARSR-4 / CRSR)

- **Name/Type:** Air Route Surveillance Radar (ARSR-4) and Common Route Surveillance Radar (CRSR)
- **Owner/Operator:** Federal Aviation Administration (in coordination with DOD for homeland defense)
- **Description:** Long-range radar systems with detection range of 200–250 nautical miles and altitude detection up to 100,000 feet. ARSR-4s are positioned around the country's perimeter; CRSR systems cover the interior.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Provide broad coverage at higher altitudes for en-route air traffic. These are co-located with short-range systems and can detect larger, higher-altitude objects.
- **Limitations:** Equally spaced for homeland defense/ATC coverage, not optimized for UAP detection. Line-of-sight limitations due to Earth's curvature at low altitudes. Not tasked or operated in modes designed to detect UAP.
- **Who Referenced It:** Mike Freie

### 2.3 ADS-B (Automatic Dependent Surveillance–Broadcast)

- **Name/Type:** Cooperative surveillance broadcast system
- **Owner/Operator:** Federal Aviation Administration (equipment mandated on aircraft)
- **Description:** Aircraft-equipped transponders that broadcast position, altitude, velocity, and identity data. Primary and preferred data source for FAA airspace management.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Provides excellent coverage of cooperative (transponder-equipped) aircraft down to ~1,500 feet AGL across the U.S. Critical for distinguishing known air traffic from potential UAP—if an object has ADS-B, it is identified.
- **Limitations:** By definition, only tracks cooperative targets. UAPs by nature would not be broadcasting ADS-B. Not relevant for non-cooperative target detection.
- **Who Referenced It:** Mike Freie

### 2.4 Air Traffic Control Radar Data

- **Name/Type:** Processed radar track data from the FAA's air traffic control system
- **Owner/Operator:** Federal Aviation Administration
- **Description:** Combined cooperative and non-cooperative surveillance data merged into controller displays showing aircraft position, altitude, and identity.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Kirkpatrick described how air traffic control data was used to match three "UAP" dots from the P-3 video to commercial aircraft on a major flight corridor heading into a major airport. This demonstrates the critical value of FAA data for resolving UAP reports.
- **Limitations:** Data is processed and filtered (not raw radar returns). Filtering settings are optimized for manned aircraft and may exclude slow-moving, small, or low-altitude objects. Raw IQ data volumes are enormous and not currently retained.
- **Who Referenced It:** Sean Kirkpatrick, Mike Freie

### 2.5 ERAM / STAR Display Systems

- **Name/Type:** En Route Automation Modernization (ERAM) and Standard Terminal Automation Replacement System (STARS)
- **Owner/Operator:** Federal Aviation Administration
- **Description:** The automation and display systems used in en-route centers (ERAM) and terminal TRACON facilities (STARS) to present surveillance data to air traffic controllers.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Freie noted that retained FAA data is effectively the processed data as displayed on these systems—not raw radar signals. This is the form in which archived data exists.
- **Limitations:** Retention period measured in months. Data has already been filtered and processed, losing raw signal information. Walter Scott specifically asked about raw vs. processed data; Freie confirmed only processed forms are retained.
- **Who Referenced It:** Mike Freie, Walter Scott

### 2.6 FAA Domestic Event Network (DEN)

- **Name/Type:** Domestic Events Network — internal FAA reporting channel
- **Owner/Operator:** Federal Aviation Administration
- **Description:** An internal FAA communication function through which air traffic controllers can report unusual observations, including UAP sightings.
- **Relevance to UAP:** The formal mechanism by which controller UAP reports are filed. Historically 3–5 reports/month across all 14,000+ controllers. Uptick to 8–10/month in August 2022 (possibly due to Starlink launches) and 68 reports starting in February 2023 (Chinese balloon incident).
- **Limitations:** Extremely low volume relative to the number of operations. Freie was unsure of the specific process for pilot reporting. No clear mechanism to encourage or incentivize reporting. Possible stigma effects not addressed by the process.
- **Who Referenced It:** Mike Freie, Mike Gold

### 2.7 Commercial Pilot Eyewitness Reports

- **Name/Type:** First-person accounts from commercial airline pilots of anomalous aerial observations
- **Owner/Operator:** Commercial airlines; reports channeled through FAA or military reporting systems
- **Description:** Visual observations by trained commercial aviators of unidentified objects or phenomena during flight operations.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Spergel highlighted that commercial pilots are "very reluctant to report anomalies" due to stigma. These reports represent a potentially valuable but severely underutilized data stream.
- **Limitations:** Spergel described eyewitness reports alone as "insufficient to provide conclusive evidence." Reports are often "uninformative due to lack of quality control and data curation." Significant underreporting due to stigma and professional risk.
- **Who Referenced It:** David Spergel, Nadia Drake, Karlin Toner

## 3. Space-Based Assets

### 3.1 NASA Earth-Sensing Satellites

- **Name/Type:** NASA's fleet of Earth-observing satellites (Earth Science Division)
- **Owner/Operator:** NASA
- **Description:** Satellites measuring atmospheric, oceanic, terrestrial, and radiological phenomena from orbit with rigorously calibrated instruments.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Kirkpatrick specifically recommended evaluating whether any earth-sensing satellites could detect UAP given the "typical UAP target" profile. NASA's Fox invited the public to explore data.nasa.gov with "tens of thousands of data sets." Bontempi noted the archive could be mined for environmental conditions around reported UAP events.
- **Limitations:** Data was not collected for UAP detection, introducing bias. Spergel noted NASA's current assets would see "really big haystacks that are moving very slowly." Frame rates likely too low for fast-moving objects. Shelley Wright identified frame rate as a major limitation for detecting small, fast UAP.
- **Who Referenced It:** Sean Kirkpatrick, Nicola Fox, Dr. Paula Bontempi, Shelley Wright, Walter Scott

### 3.2 NOAA Satellites & Sensors

- **Name/Type:** National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration weather and environmental satellites
- **Owner/Operator:** NOAA (Department of Commerce)
- **Description:** Operational weather, climate, and environmental monitoring satellites providing continuous atmospheric and oceanic observations.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Kirkpatrick listed NOAA sensors alongside NASA sensors as potential data sources for evaluating UAP detections. Bontempi identified NOAA as a federal partner whose data could help understand reported phenomena.
- **Limitations:** Designed for weather/climate monitoring, not for detecting small, fast-moving objects. Similar resolution and frame-rate constraints as NASA Earth-observing satellites.
- **Who Referenced It:** Sean Kirkpatrick, Dr. Paula Bontempi

### 3.3 Hubble Space Telescope

- **Name/Type:** Optical/UV space telescope
- **Owner/Operator:** NASA / ESA / Space Telescope Science Institute
- **Description:** 2.4-meter space telescope operating since 1990, observing in visible, ultraviolet, and near-infrared light.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Spergel used Hubble as an example of why instrument characterization matters—pointing Hubble at the Sun would destroy its detectors, and off-axis light creates "ghosting" artifacts. This illustrates why understanding instrument behavior is essential to interpreting anomalous images.
- **Limitations:** Designed for deep-space astronomical observation, not Earth monitoring. Off-axis reflections, saturation bleeding, and diffraction artifacts can create misleading images.
- **Who Referenced It:** David Spergel

### 3.4 James Webb Space Telescope

- **Name/Type:** Infrared space telescope
- **Owner/Operator:** NASA / ESA / CSA
- **Description:** 6.5-meter infrared space telescope launched in 2021 for deep-space observation.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Referenced alongside Hubble to illustrate that even world-class instruments produce "funny" phenomena (diffraction spikes, saturation, dust scattering) when observing bright sources—underscoring that anomalous images often have mundane instrumental explanations.
- **Limitations:** Same as Hubble—designed for astronomical observation; instrumental artifacts can mimic anomalous signals.
- **Who Referenced It:** David Spergel

### 3.5 International Space Station (ISS) Cameras

- **Name/Type:** High-speed and standard imaging cameras aboard the ISS
- **Owner/Operator:** NASA / International Partners
- **Description:** Various camera systems aboard the ISS used for Earth observation, documentation, and scientific experiments.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Spergel referenced ISS imaging as the platform that finally confirmed the existence of sprites (upward-going lightning)—a phenomenon reported by commercial pilots for years but dismissed until high-speed imaging from space captured it definitively.
- **Limitations:** Incidental observation capability; not designed for systematic atmospheric anomaly detection.
- **Who Referenced It:** David Spergel

### 3.6 NASA Planetary Science Mission Instruments

- **Name/Type:** Instruments aboard NASA planetary exploration missions (orbiters, landers, flyby spacecraft)
- **Owner/Operator:** NASA
- **Description:** Scientific instruments designed to detect biosignatures, characterize planetary atmospheres, and search for anomalies in the solar system.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Grinspoon argued that NASA could leverage existing planetary mission instruments to search for objects with anomalous motion, trajectories, light curves, or spectral signatures within the solar system. Most of the solar system has not been searched for artifacts or anomalies.
- **Limitations:** Instruments designed for planetary science, not for detecting fast-moving or transient objects near Earth. Coverage of the solar system is sparse and non-comprehensive.
- **Who Referenced It:** David Grinspoon

### 3.7 Commercial Satellite Constellations

- **Name/Type:** Earth-observation and communication satellite constellations operated by private companies
- **Owner/Operator:** Various commercial satellite operators (e.g., SpaceX Starlink, Planet Labs, Maxar, etc.)
- **Description:** Privately operated satellites conducting persistent Earth observation, communications, and other services.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Fox noted "a lot of satellite companies that are doing that persistent collection as well." Jennifer Buss noted commercial space companies "have troves of data" and lobbied to present to the panel. Gold highlighted the emerging commercial space transportation sector from LEO to cislunar space.
- **Limitations:** Data collected for purposes other than UAP detection. Well-calibrated systems but only one source among many needed. Commercial proprietary restrictions may limit access.
- **Who Referenced It:** Nicola Fox, Jennifer Buss, Mike Gold

## 4. Ground-Based Scientific Instruments

### 4.1 Large-Scale Radio Observatories

- **Name/Type:** Large radio telescope arrays and single-dish radio observatories
- **Owner/Operator:** NSF, academia, international consortia (e.g., Parkes Observatory, Australia)
- **Description:** Sensitive radio frequency detectors designed for astronomical observation, capable of detecting transient radio signals.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Spergel told the story of Fast Radio Bursts (FRBs)—some of which were real astrophysical phenomena and some caused by a microwave oven at the Australian observatory. This illustrates how large-scale scientific instruments not designed for UAP detection could coincidentally detect anomalous signals. Kirkpatrick directly recommended evaluating such instruments against AARO's "surrogate target package."
- **Limitations:** Not designed or pointed for UAP detection. False positives from terrestrial sources (microwave ovens, cell phones, etc.) are common. Kirkpatrick noted the need to model whether these instruments "have a chance of picking anything up that would be helpful."
- **Who Referenced It:** David Spergel, Sean Kirkpatrick

### 4.2 Astrophysical & Geographical Observatories

- **Name/Type:** Ground-based astronomical and Earth-science observatories
- **Owner/Operator:** NSF, NASA, universities, international partners
- **Description:** Professional-grade observatories including optical, infrared, and multi-spectral facilities. Some modern observatories are designed for time-domain anomaly detection (transients) and near-Earth object tracking.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Bianco specifically recommended leveraging existing astrophysical and geographical observatories—both ground and satellite—for UAP detection. She noted that some modern observatories are "designed for the detection of time anomalies specifically, as well as for the detection of objects that move rapidly in space."
- **Limitations:** Pointed at the sky or Earth surface, not optimized for atmospheric UAP detection. May lack the frame rate or spectral coverage needed for fast-moving objects.
- **Who Referenced It:** Federica Bianco, Sean Kirkpatrick

### 4.3 Near-Earth Object (NEO) Detection Facilities

- **Name/Type:** Ground- and space-based facilities for detecting and tracking asteroids, comets, and other near-Earth objects
- **Owner/Operator:** NASA / Academia / International partners
- **Description:** Telescopes and survey systems designed to catalog and track objects in near-Earth space, including potentially hazardous asteroids.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Grinspoon noted NASA already has an active NEO detection program that could be leveraged to search for objects with anomalous trajectories or characteristics in near-Earth space. Gold mentioned Apophis as an example of an NEO that will pass below geosynchronous satellites, illustrating the need for comprehensive orbital monitoring.
- **Limitations:** Focused on objects in solar orbit, not atmospheric phenomena. Detection cadence may be too slow for fast-moving objects.
- **Who Referenced It:** David Grinspoon, Mike Gold, Federica Bianco

### 4.4 NASA Ground-Based Sensor Networks

- **Name/Type:** Various NASA ground-based monitoring and detection systems
- **Owner/Operator:** NASA
- **Description:** Ground networks including radiological sensors, atmospheric monitoring stations, and other Earth-science instruments.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Kirkpatrick listed NASA's "radiological sensors, tools for gravitational wave, geomagnetic wave detection" as capabilities that may assist UAP efforts.
- **Limitations:** Not designed for UAP detection; would require evaluation of whether any could detect relevant UAP signatures.
- **Who Referenced It:** Sean Kirkpatrick

### 4.5 Radiological / Gravitational Wave / Geomagnetic Sensors

- **Name/Type:** Specialized scientific detectors for radiological, gravitational-wave, and geomagnetic phenomena
- **Owner/Operator:** NASA, DOE National Laboratories, NSF (e.g., LIGO)
- **Description:** High-sensitivity instruments measuring radiation, spacetime distortions, and Earth's magnetic field variations.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Kirkpatrick specifically listed these among NASA's unique capabilities. He recommended applying AI/ML tools to search archived data from these instruments for anomalous signatures correlating with UAP reporting.
- **Limitations:** Extremely specialized; unclear what UAP-related signatures they might detect. Not pointed at the sky for visual/kinematic observations.
- **Who Referenced It:** Sean Kirkpatrick

## 5. Crowdsource / Public Data

### 5.1 Smartphone / iPhone Imagery & Metadata

- **Name/Type:** Consumer smartphone cameras and associated sensor metadata (GPS, accelerometer, gyroscope, compass, timestamp)
- **Owner/Operator:** General public (~4 billion smartphones globally)
- **Description:** Ubiquitous mobile devices with cameras and multiple built-in sensors capable of capturing imagery and rich contextual metadata.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Kirkpatrick noted that while *"imagery from an iPhone is generally not helpful unless you are right up on whatever it is you're looking at,"* the ancillary metadata (location, speed, time, and "more than one of those [phenomenologies]") *"can be very helpful."* Spergel called smartphones "fabulous data collectors" and proposed NASA develop an app for uniform, centralized UAP data collection.
- **Limitations:** Image quality typically poor for distant objects. Single-camera perspective limits parallax correction. Spergel acknowledged most collected data would "turn out to be commercial planes, balloons."
- **Who Referenced It:** Sean Kirkpatrick, David Spergel, Federica Bianco, Walter Scott

### 5.2 Crowdsourcing Data Platform (Proposed)

- **Name/Type:** Proposed centralized platform for public UAP data collection and coordination
- **Owner/Operator:** NASA (proposed role)
- **Description:** A hypothetical platform enabling the public to submit UAP observations with standardized data and metadata, enabling coordinated multi-observer follow-up and centralized curation.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Bianco advocated strongly for such a platform, stating that "a set of guidance of best practices would not be sufficient" and that a full platform supporting data collection, metadata capture, transmission, and coordination is needed. She proposed NASA could play a key role in development. Spergel supported this with a proposed NASA app.
- **Limitations:** Does not yet exist. Bianco noted the challenge of reaching "a large enough community to really have a crowd to source the problem."
- **Who Referenced It:** Federica Bianco, David Spergel, Walter Scott, Karlin Toner

### 5.3 Social Media & Search Trend Data

- **Name/Type:** Social media posts, search engine trend data, and online activity patterns
- **Owner/Operator:** Various platforms (Google, Twitter/X, Facebook, etc.)
- **Description:** Aggregated online behavior data that can indicate where unusual events are occurring in real time.
- **Relevance to UAP:** A panelist suggested using social media and Google search surges—similar to how disease outbreaks are tracked—to identify areas of UAP activity and queue sensors to those locations in real time.
- **Limitations:** Bianco objected that such approaches may introduce noise and bias. Data is not scientific in nature.
- **Who Referenced It:** Panelist (Speaker 4), Federica Bianco

### 5.4 Citizen Science Eyewitness Reports

- **Name/Type:** Observations and reports from members of the general public
- **Owner/Operator:** General public; aggregated by various organizations
- **Description:** First-person accounts of UAP sightings submitted by civilians, including location, time, and narrative descriptions.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Spergel described existing UAP data as "in many ways what we'd like to think of as citizen science." Bianco noted eyewitness reports may contain important information about "persistent sighting locations or seasonality" but only work when joined with quantitative sensor data. Drake noted eyewitness reports "on their own can be interesting and compelling, but often lack the information needed to make definitive conclusions."
- **Limitations:** Spergel: "insufficient to provide conclusive evidence about the nature and origin" of UAP. "Often uninformative due to lack of quality control and data curation." Bianco recommended they be supplemented with biophysical and psychophysical assessments of witnesses.
- **Who Referenced It:** David Spergel, Nadia Drake, Federica Bianco, Karlin Toner

## 6. Databases / Archives

### 6.1 AARO UAP Case Database

- **Name/Type:** Classified and unclassified database of reported UAP events and case files
- **Owner/Operator:** AARO (All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office), U.S. Department of Defense
- **Description:** Central repository of UAP reports from military, intelligence, and civilian sources. As of the meeting, contained 800+ cases spanning ~27 years, growing by 50–100 new reports per month. Only 2–5% display potentially anomalous signatures.
- **Relevance to UAP:** The primary government database for UAP analysis. Kirkpatrick presented trend analyses showing most reports are at aircraft altitudes, ~50% are orbs/spheres, and reporting is geographically biased toward sensor-rich military areas. Most unresolved cases are due to lack of data, not true anomalousness.
- **Limitations:** Majority of data is classified. Geographic distribution is "a collection biased map" reflecting sensor placement, not actual UAP distribution. No space or maritime reports. Kirkpatrick: "Without sufficient data, we are unable to reach defendable conclusions."
- **Who Referenced It:** Sean Kirkpatrick, Nadia Drake

### 6.2 NASA Open Data Portal (data.nasa.gov)

- **Name/Type:** Public, open-access data repository for all NASA mission and science data
- **Owner/Operator:** NASA
- **Description:** Online portal providing free access to tens of thousands of datasets from NASA's Earth science, space science, aeronautics, and human exploration missions.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Fox explicitly invited the public to "visit our open data portal at data.nasa.gov to comb through our tens of thousands of data sets that are free and fully accessible to the public." Spergel noted NASA has "a lot of experience in serving as a clearing house for data from across the government for civilian data."
- **Limitations:** Data not collected for UAP purposes. Requires domain expertise to interpret. Bontempi noted "not all data in an archive are user-friendly right out of the gate."
- **Who Referenced It:** Nicola Fox, David Spergel

### 6.3 Federal Open Data Portal (data.gov)

- **Name/Type:** Government-wide open data portal
- **Owner/Operator:** U.S. Federal Government
- **Description:** Central portal aggregating open datasets from all federal agencies, including links to science and mission data archives.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Fox directed the public to "data.gov/open/data for a great overview of where you can find the archives for our science and mission data sites."
- **Limitations:** Broad portal requiring users to navigate to specific agency datasets.
- **Who Referenced It:** Nicola Fox

### 6.4 NASA Archival Mission Data

- **Name/Type:** Historical and current data from all NASA science and exploration missions
- **Owner/Operator:** NASA
- **Description:** Decades of calibrated, well-documented data from satellites, space telescopes, planetary probes, aeronautics research, and Earth science missions. Includes rigorous calibration protocols and "Quick Look" preliminary data products.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Bontempi highlighted the "tremendous archive of NASA data" from satellites, space-based, and ground networks. Bianco noted NASA has "recently spearheaded an effort to review NASA and NASA partner archival data to prepare them for machine learning and AI." Kirkpatrick recommended applying AI/ML to search archived scientific data for anomalous signatures.
- **Limitations:** Spergel noted that archival data from years past is "very hard to know what's taken" and "very hard to draw conclusions." Not collected for UAP identification. Fox distinguished "Quick Look" data from fully calibrated science-grade data.
- **Who Referenced It:** Dr. Paula Bontempi, Federica Bianco, Sean Kirkpatrick, Nicola Fox, David Spergel

### 6.5 FAA Radar Data Archives

- **Name/Type:** Retained processed radar data from FAA air traffic control systems
- **Owner/Operator:** Federal Aviation Administration
- **Description:** Archived surveillance data from ERAM and STARS display systems, retained for a period measured in months (for legal/incident investigation purposes).
- **Relevance to UAP:** Spergel identified FAA radar data as "one [dataset] we don't want to forget about as an available dataset." Could be cross-referenced with UAP sighting reports to determine if known air traffic explains events.
- **Limitations:** Retention period is limited (months). Only processed data is retained, not raw radar returns. Raw IQ data is "huge—gigabyte, very large volumes" and would be "cost prohibitive" to store. Filtering settings may have already removed potential UAP signals.
- **Who Referenced It:** Mike Freie, David Spergel, Walter Scott, Reggie Brothers

### 6.6 Archived Scientific Instrument Data (Global)

- **Name/Type:** Calibrated data archives from large-scale scientific instruments worldwide
- **Owner/Operator:** NSF, international research institutions, universities
- **Description:** Decades of data from radio telescopes, optical observatories, atmospheric monitoring stations, and other scientific facilities around the globe.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Kirkpatrick specifically recommended: *"We have a whole bunch of calibrated large scale scientific data from all these different instrumentations around the world. Taking a look at how can you apply maybe some AI/ML tools to search through that data for anomalous signatures that may correlate to things that we've got reporting on."*
- **Limitations:** Vast volumes requiring automated analysis. Instruments not designed or pointed for UAP detection. Data formats and standards vary across institutions.
- **Who Referenced It:** Sean Kirkpatrick

### 6.7 UAP Tracking Organization Records

- **Name/Type:** Databases maintained by nonprofit and for-profit organizations that track UAP sightings
- **Owner/Operator:** Various civilian UAP research organizations (e.g., MUFON, NUFORC, The Galileo Project, etc.)
- **Description:** Collections of UAP reports, sightings, and analyses compiled by civilian organizations over many years.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Jennifer Buss reviewed these sources and concluded that "much of the inputs collected by these organizations are not considered scientific data in nature" and "do not contain unbiased information, they're not repeatable." However, distributions of sightings from these organizations could be combined with AARO's classified map to create "a holistic picture."
- **Limitations:** Buss: data is "not considered scientific data in nature," typically comes with eyewitness accounts only, not repeatable. Spergel noted current data is "uncalibrated, poorly characterized, not well curated."
- **Who Referenced It:** Jennifer Buss, Sean Kirkpatrick, David Spergel

### 6.8 SETI Radio & Optical Signal Archives

- **Name/Type:** Archives of radio and optical signals collected by SETI (Search for Extraterrestrial Intelligence) programs
- **Owner/Operator:** SETI Institute, universities, international radio observatories
- **Description:** Decades of radio and optical observations searching for potential extraterrestrial signals, with established protocols for distinguishing real signals from terrestrial interference and natural phenomena.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Grinspoon highlighted that the SETI community has "expertise in methodologies for determining whether a potential technosignature is in fact a natural phenomenon or misinterpreted terrestrial technology." A panelist noted SETI has "a lot of data there that we've been collecting" with well-standardized datasets amenable to machine learning.
- **Limitations:** Focused on intentional signals rather than incidental technosignatures. Grinspoon noted the distinction between classical SETI (looking for deliberate signals) and the broader technosignature concept.
- **Who Referenced It:** David Grinspoon, Panelist (Speaker 5)

### 6.9 Biosignature / Technosignature Databases

- **Name/Type:** Standardized databases of biological and technological signatures used in astrobiology research
- **Owner/Operator:** NASA, academia, SETI Institute
- **Description:** Well-characterized datasets of atmospheric gases, spectral signatures, and other indicators that may reveal biological or technological activity on other planets.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Grinspoon drew parallels between biosignature/technosignature detection and UAP investigation. A panelist noted these fields "have very well standardized data sets" and "have been collecting data sets for some time, and they are able to apply machine learning, artificial intelligence algorithms"—a model for how UAP data should aspire to be organized.
- **Limitations:** Technosignature research is relatively new and data collection has been limited compared to biosignature studies. The fields are still developing standardized frameworks.
- **Who Referenced It:** David Grinspoon, Panelist (Speaker 5), Dr. Paula Bontempi

### 6.10 DOD & DOE Lab Historical UAP Records

- **Name/Type:** Historical records and analyses of UAP observations from Department of Defense and Department of Energy national laboratories
- **Owner/Operator:** DOD, DOE
- **Description:** Classified records and physics-based analyses of current and historical UAP observations, including assessments of maneuverability, speed, signature management, and propulsion characteristics.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Kirkpatrick described partnerships with "both DOD and DOE labs to explore our current state-of-the-art fundamental physics of UAP observations, both current and historical." These records inform understanding of whether observed characteristics are physically plausible.
- **Limitations:** Data is classified. Kirkpatrick noted the work "will take time" and requires peer review before conclusions can be drawn.
- **Who Referenced It:** Sean Kirkpatrick

### 6.11 National Weather Service Balloon Tracking Data

- **Name/Type:** Tracking data from twice-daily weather balloon launches
- **Owner/Operator:** National Weather Service (NOAA)
- **Description:** GPS and radiosonde tracking data from 92 U.S. weather stations releasing balloons twice daily (0000 and 1200 Zulu), ascending to ~100,000 feet over ~2-hour flights. At least 184 flights daily, excluding university and hobbyist launches.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Freie provided these statistics as a key component of the "background" environment that must be characterized to distinguish UAP from known objects. Worldwide, ~1,600 weather balloon launches per day. Kirkpatrick proposed flying F-35s against weather balloons at various conditions to calibrate sensors.
- **Limitations:** Known and tracked, but can be visually misidentified. Scott Kelly recounted a Navy aviator's conviction they had seen a UFO that turned out to be a Bart Simpson balloon.
- **Who Referenced It:** Mike Freie, Nadia Drake, Sean Kirkpatrick, Scott Kelly
