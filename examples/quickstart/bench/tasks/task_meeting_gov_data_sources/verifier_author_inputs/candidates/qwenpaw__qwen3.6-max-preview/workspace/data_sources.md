## Government/Military Sensors

### 1. DOD Military Sensors (General)

- **Owner/Operator:** U.S. Department of Defense
- **Description:** Broad category of military sensor systems used for detection, tracking, and targeting of airborne objects. Includes radar, electro-optical, infrared, and other sensor types mounted on aircraft, ships, and ground stations.
- **Relevance to UAP:** The vast majority of UAP reports in the AARO database come from DOD sensors. Sean Kirkpatrick emphasized that these sensors are the primary source of UAP data. The sensors create the "collection bias" visible in the AARO heat map of sightings.
- **Limitations:** DOD sensors are **not scientific sensors**. As Kirkpatrick stated: *"DOD sensors have one purpose. They are to identify an object that is known and put a weapon on it."* They are not calibrated for scientific data collection, lack precision for characterization, and are not optimized for detecting small, slow-moving, or anomalous objects. They are designed for known threat identification, not anomaly detection.
- **Referenced by:** Sean Kirkpatrick, David Spergel, Reggie Brothers

### 2. Intelligence Community (IC) Sensors

- **Owner/Operator:** U.S. Intelligence Community
- **Description:** Classified sensor platforms used for intelligence gathering, including spy satellites and other advanced collection systems.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Kirkpatrick noted that IC sensors are much closer to scientific sensors than DOD sensors: *"Intelligence community sensors are very close to scientific sensors. They are calibrated, they are high precision, they are everything you'd ever want to know about a thing."*
- **Limitations:** Data from IC sensors is classified, primarily because the sensor capabilities themselves are classified—not because of what is being observed. As David Spergel noted: *"If a spy satellite takes an image of a balloon... that data is classified... because of a desire to not reveal our technical capabilities to other nations."* This limits open scientific analysis.
- **Referenced by:** Sean Kirkpatrick, David Spergel, Nicola Fox

### 3. F-35 Fighter Jet Sensors

- **Owner/Operator:** U.S. Department of Defense
- **Description:** Advanced multi-spectral sensor suite on the F-35 Joint Strike Fighter, including electro-optical targeting systems, radar, and infrared sensors.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Used as an example of how military sensors can capture UAP imagery. David Spergel used the F-35 to illustrate the classification issue: *"If a camera on an F-35 took a picture of a bird, it's classified."* Kirkpatrick also mentioned using F-35s for calibration flights against known objects like weather balloons.
- **Limitations:** Sensors are designed for targeting, not scientific observation. Not calibrated for UAP detection. Sensor artifacts, gimbal jitter, and parallax effects can create misleading impressions. Calibration against known objects at various conditions is needed.
- **Referenced by:** David Spergel, Sean Kirkpatrick

### 4. MQ-9 Reaper EO/IR Sensor

- **Owner/Operator:** U.S. Department of Defense
- **Description:** Electro-optical (EO) sensor mounted on the MQ-9 Reaper unmanned aerial vehicle. Used for surveillance and target identification.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Captured the "spherical orb metallic" UAP footage in the Middle East in 2022 that Kirkpatrick showed during his presentation. This represents the most commonly reported UAP morphology (orbs/spheres).
- **Limitations:** Kirkpatrick confirmed the sensor is well understood (*"we understand that very, very well"*) and the object was real, but the sensor was not designed for scientific characterization. Limited ability to determine size, speed, or composition from a single sensor at unknown range. Walter Scott questioned whether sensor or data processing artifacts had been ruled out.
- **Referenced by:** Sean Kirkpatrick, Walter Scott

### 5. P-3 Orion Sensor System

- **Owner/Operator:** U.S. Navy
- **Description:** Sensor suite on the P-3 maritime patrol aircraft, used for surveillance and tracking.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Captured the newly declassified video showing three "dots" moving back and forth that was initially reported as a UAP grouping. After analysis with air traffic control data, the objects were identified as commercial aircraft on a flight corridor.
- **Limitations:** The apparent motion of the dots was caused by sensor/platform jitter, not actual UAP movement. Kirkpatrick explained: *"The moving back and forth is from the sensor and the platform that's collecting it."* The objects were 30-40 miles away, and the sensor was not stabilized against the target. Demonstrates how sensor artifacts can create false impressions of anomalous behavior.
- **Referenced by:** Sean Kirkpatrick, Nicola Fox

### 6. AARO Purpose-Built Sensors

- **Owner/Operator:** AARO (All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office, DoD)
- **Description:** Dedicated sensors purpose-built by AARO to detect, track, and characterize UAP. Designed based on the "typical UAP target package" (1-4 meter objects, 0-Mach 2 speed range).
- **Relevance to UAP:** Kirkpatrick described these as part of AARO's scientific plan: *"We are augmenting with dedicated sensors that we've purpose built designed to detect, track and characterize those particular objects. We will then be putting those out in very select areas for surveillance purposes."* These represent the first sensors specifically designed for UAP detection rather than repurposed military equipment.
- **Limitations:** Still in development/deployment phase. Kirkpatrick described the approach as a "bootstrap method" — starting with broad spectrum search across few indicators, then refining. Limited deployment to select areas. Legal constraints prevent pointing collection apparatus at domestic locations without justification.
- **Referenced by:** Sean Kirkpatrick, Reggie Brothers

### 7. Five Eyes Partner Sensors

- **Owner/Operator:** United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, and United States
- **Description:** Sensor systems operated by Five Eyes intelligence alliance partner nations.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Kirkpatrick announced the first Five Eyes forum on UAP had been held, with discussions on data sharing, reporting, analysis, and calibration assistance. International sensor data could provide a more holistic global picture of UAP distributions beyond U.S. national security areas.
- **Limitations:** Partnership framework still being established. Data sharing protocols and standardization not yet fully developed. Kirkpatrick noted he lacked time/bandwidth to expand beyond Five Eyes and looked to NASA to broaden scientific and academic relationships with allies.
- **Referenced by:** Sean Kirkpatrick

## Civilian Aviation Systems

### 8. FAA Short-Range Radars (Airport Surveillance Radar — ASR)

- **Owner/Operator:** Federal Aviation Administration
- **Description:** Short-range radars located at high and medium density airports. Detection range: 40-60 miles. Altitude detection: up to ~24,000 feet. Rotating fixed-face radars operating at 12 RPM (terminal surveillance).
- **Relevance to UAP:** Part of the FAA's surveillance infrastructure that could potentially detect UAP in the vicinity of airports. Mike Freie presented coverage maps showing these sensors (green, blue, and pink markers) concentrated in the eastern U.S. where population and airport density is higher.
- **Limitations:** Designed for manned aircraft detection, not small objects. Detection assumes a 1-square-meter radar cross-section target. A small UAS (drone) at 0.01 square meters would be 100 times harder to detect than a fighter jet. Coverage is limited at low altitudes, especially in the western U.S. due to mountain screening. Filters are applied to remove ground clutter, insects, dust, and biologicals — which could also filter out UAP. Raw data is not retained; only processed/filtered data is stored.
- **Referenced by:** Mike Freie, Walter Scott, Warren Randolph

### 9. FAA Long-Range Radars (ARSR-4, CRSR)

- **Owner/Operator:** Federal Aviation Administration (with national defense/homeland security mission)
- **Description:** Long-range radar systems with detection range of 200-250 nautical miles and altitude detection up to 100,000 feet. ARSR-4 systems are located around the U.S. perimeter; CRSR systems are on the interior. Rotating at 5 RPM (en route surveillance).
- **Relevance to UAP:** Provide broader coverage of U.S. airspace and could detect larger UAP at higher altitudes. These systems serve dual air traffic control and homeland defense missions.
- **Limitations:** Same fundamental limitations as short-range radars: designed for manned aircraft, not small anomalous objects. Line-of-sight limited by Earth's curvature. Mountain screening in the western U.S. reduces coverage. Filters remove slow-moving and small targets. Data retained only in processed form, not raw radar returns. Retention period measured in months (for legal/accident purposes).
- **Referenced by:** Mike Freie, Walter Scott

### 10. ADS-B (Automatic Dependent Surveillance-Broadcast)

- **Owner/Operator:** Federal Aviation Administration
- **Description:** Cooperative surveillance system where aircraft broadcast their GPS position, altitude, speed, and other data. Ground stations receive these broadcasts for air traffic management.
- **Relevance to UAP:** ADS-B provides excellent coverage across the U.S. down to 1,500 feet above ground level. Freie included ADS-B coverage maps to show cooperative surveillance capability. ADS-B data can help identify and rule out known aircraft in UAP investigations.
- **Limitations:** ADS-B is a **cooperative system** — it only works for aircraft equipped with ADS-B transponders that are actively broadcasting. UAP, by definition, would not be transmitting ADS-B signals. As Freie noted: *"For the purposes of this briefing... the cooperative sensors, those are neither unknown or anomalous."* ADS-B cannot detect non-cooperative targets.
- **Referenced by:** Mike Freie

### 11. FAA TRACON Facilities (Terminal Radar Approach Control)

- **Owner/Operator:** Federal Aviation Administration
- **Description:** 147 manned air traffic control facilities at high and medium density airports managing terminal airspace.
- **Relevance to UAP:** TRACON controllers can report UAP sightings through the FAA's reporting process. These facilities manage the airspace where many UAP reports originate (near airports and at typical aircraft altitudes).
- **Limitations:** Controllers report only 3-5 UAP sightings per month across all 14,000 controllers nationwide. Stigma may impair reporting. TRACONs rely on the same radar systems with the limitations described above.
- **Referenced by:** Mike Freie

### 12. FAA Air Route Traffic Control Centers (ARTCC)

- **Owner/Operator:** Federal Aviation Administration
- **Description:** 21 centers managing en-route air traffic control across the continental United States.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Handle high-altitude en-route traffic where some UAP reports occur. Part of the overall FAA surveillance and reporting infrastructure.
- **Limitations:** Same as other FAA systems — designed for cooperative manned aircraft, not anomalous non-cooperative objects.
- **Referenced by:** Mike Freie

### 13. ERAM System (En Route Automation Modernization)

- **Owner/Operator:** Federal Aviation Administration
- **Description:** The data processing and display system used at FAA en route centers for managing air traffic.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Walter Scott asked whether retained radar data was raw or processed; Mike Freie indicated retained data is processed form displayed on systems like ERAM.
- **Limitations:** Only processed/filtered data is retained, not raw radar returns. Raw "INQ data" is described as "huge gigabyte, very large volumes" that would be "cost prohibitive" to retain. Processing and filtering may remove potential UAP signals.
- **Referenced by:** Mike Freie, Walter Scott

### 14. STAR System (Standard Terminal Automation Replacement)

- **Owner/Operator:** Federal Aviation Administration
- **Description:** The data processing and display system used at FAA terminal facilities (TRACONs) for managing air traffic.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Same as ERAM — part of the FAA's data retention infrastructure. Processed data from terminal radars is displayed and stored through this system.
- **Limitations:** Same as ERAM — only processed data retained, not raw radar data.
- **Referenced by:** Mike Freie, Walter Scott

### 15. Air Traffic Control Data / Flight Corridor Data

- **Owner/Operator:** Federal Aviation Administration
- **Description:** Comprehensive data on commercial and civilian aircraft positions, flight paths, and corridors maintained by the FAA.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Kirkpatrick described how air traffic control data was used to resolve the P-3 UAP case: *"When we went even deeper looked at air traffic control data, we were able to match those to aircraft on a major flight corridor heading into a major airport for landing."* This data is critical for ruling out known aircraft in UAP investigations. David Spergel noted FAA data as a key resource for cross-referencing citizen science reports.
- **Limitations:** Only covers cooperative, registered aircraft. Does not capture non-cooperative or unregistered objects. Integration with AARO's database was only recently completed (Kirkpatrick noted getting "FAA's data integrated in finally" resulting in 100+ new cases).
- **Referenced by:** Sean Kirkpatrick, David Spergel

## Space-Based Assets

### 16. NASA Earth Sensing Satellites

- **Owner/Operator:** NASA
- **Description:** NASA's fleet of Earth observation satellites measuring atmospheric, oceanic, land surface, and other Earth science parameters.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Kirkpatrick specifically identified NASA earth sensing satellites as potential UAP detection assets: *"Understanding if any of these earth sensing satellites... can actually see these things, given what we've got so far, is going to be an important first step."* Paula Bontempi noted NASA has "tremendous archive of data from satellites and other space-based and ground networks." These satellites provide calibrated, well-characterized data that could be searched for anomalous signatures.
- **Limitations:** Not designed or optimized for UAP detection. Frame rates, spatial resolution, and spectral bands may not be suitable for detecting small (1-4 meter), fast-moving objects. Shelley Wright noted that NASA would "likely have to increase its frame rate into its detectors" to catch fast-moving objects. Walter noted current assets would see "really big haystacks that are moving very slowly."
- **Referenced by:** Sean Kirkpatrick, Paula Bontempi, Shelley Wright, Walter Scott, Nicola Fox

### 17. NOAA Sensors/Satellites

- **Owner/Operator:** National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA)
- **Description:** NOAA's weather, climate, and environmental monitoring satellites and ground-based sensors.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Kirkpatrick mentioned NOAA sensors alongside NASA sensors as part of the civil government sensor network that could be evaluated for UAP detection capability. Paula Bontempi also noted NOAA as a potential partner agency.
- **Limitations:** Same as NASA earth sensing satellites — not designed for UAP detection. Resolution and frame rate may be insufficient for small, fast-moving objects.
- **Referenced by:** Sean Kirkpatrick, Paula Bontempi

### 18. James Webb Space Telescope (JWST)

- **Owner/Operator:** NASA
- **Description:** NASA's flagship infrared space telescope, designed for deep-space observations of the early universe, exoplanets, and cosmic phenomena.
- **Relevance to UAP:** David Spergel referenced JWST as an example of a well-calibrated scientific instrument to illustrate sensor artifacts: *"If you look at data taken from the James Webb Telescope... you'll see some really at first funny phenomenon. When a telescope points at a star that's bright, it saturates the detector and puts bleeding down the detector."* Used to demonstrate how even the best instruments produce artifacts that could be mistaken for anomalies.
- **Limitations:** Not relevant for UAP detection — pointed away from Earth at deep space. Referenced only as an analogy for understanding sensor artifacts and the importance of well-characterized instruments.
- **Referenced by:** David Spergel

### 19. Hubble Space Telescope

- **Owner/Operator:** NASA
- **Description:** NASA's visible/ultraviolet/near-infrared space telescope in low Earth orbit.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Spergel used Hubble as an example of instrument limitations: *"You would never take the Hubble Telescope and point it at the sun, this would destroy its detectors."* Also referenced for its ghosting and diffraction artifacts when bright sources are near the field of view. Demonstrates the importance of understanding how data was collected before drawing conclusions about anomalies.
- **Limitations:** Not designed for Earth observation or UAP detection. Referenced only as an analogy for sensor calibration and artifact understanding.
- **Referenced by:** David Spergel

### 20. International Space Station (ISS) Observations

- **Owner/Operator:** NASA / International Partners
- **Description:** Manned orbital platform with cameras and observation equipment.
- **Relevance to UAP:** David Spergel referenced ISS observations of sprites (upward-going lightning): *"It was really only when we were able to take very high speed imaging data, data taken often from places like Space Station, that we're able to see and learn about these fascinating ionospheric phenomenon."* Demonstrates how space-based platforms can capture atmospheric phenomena that were previously dismissed as pilot misperception.
- **Limitations:** Not specifically tasked for UAP observation. ISS cameras were able to capture sprites because of high-speed imaging capability — a feature that may be needed for UAP detection as well.
- **Referenced by:** David Spergel

### 21. Space Shuttle Cameras

- **Owner/Operator:** NASA
- **Description:** Cameras aboard the Space Shuttle used for documentation and observation.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Scott Kelly recounted a story from his brother Mark Kelly (STS-124 commander, 2008): before closing the payload bay doors, the crew spotted an unknown object in the bay. Using a camera to photograph and zoom in, they discovered it was actually the International Space Station 80 miles away — not a tool or bolt as initially feared. This illustrates the extreme difficulty of judging distance and size from space-based imagery.
- **Limitations:** Demonstrates how optical illusions and lack of depth perception in space can lead to dramatic misidentification. A camera without range information cannot determine object size or distance.
- **Referenced by:** Scott Kelly

### 22. Commercial Satellite Company Data

- **Owner/Operator:** Various commercial satellite operators
- **Description:** Data from commercial Earth observation satellite companies that maintain persistent collection capabilities.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Jennifer Buss (Nicola Fox reading) noted: *"There are a lot of satellite companies that are doing that persistent collection as well."* Commercial companies lobbied to present to the panel and have "troves of data." Federica Bianco noted commercial space companies have well-calibrated systems.
- **Limitations:** Jennifer Buss noted: *"Those systems, while they are well calibrated, are only one source of many that could be used."* Data collected for purposes other than UAP detection. Access and data sharing arrangements may be limited by commercial considerations. Resolution and revisit rates may not be suitable for UAP detection.
- **Referenced by:** Jennifer Buss (Nicola Fox), Federica Bianco

## Ground-Based Scientific Instruments

### 23. Radio Observatories (Fast Radio Burst Detection)

- **Owner/Operator:** Various (e.g., Parkes Observatory in Australia)
- **Description:** Large radio telescope arrays designed for astronomical observations, including detection of fast radio bursts (FRBs) — powerful, brief radio signals from cosmological distances.
- **Relevance to UAP:** David Spergel used FRB discovery as a key analogy for UAP research. FRBs were discovered by instruments "very sensitive but not optimized for this." Some initial "bursts" turned out to be microwave oven interference from observatory staff opening the microwave door early — a perfect example of sensor artifacts mimicking anomalous signals. The lesson: *"Some events end up being something unusual and conventional, but some events... turn out to be these powerful explosions."* Kirkpatrick also referenced this: *"The FRB example was perfect... You have a bunch of large scale instruments that were not designed for that. Yet, they picked them up."*
- **Limitations:** Not designed for UAP detection. The microwave oven story illustrates how sensitive instruments can pick up mundane interference that appears anomalous. Requires careful calibration and understanding of background to distinguish real anomalies from artifacts.
- **Referenced by:** David Spergel, Sean Kirkpatrick

### 24. Astrophysical Observatories

- **Owner/Operator:** NSF, DOE, NASA, international partners
- **Description:** Ground-based and space-based observatories designed for detecting time-domain anomalies, near-Earth objects, asteroids, meteors, and other transient astronomical phenomena.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Federica Bianco noted: *"Some modern observatories, in particular, are designed for the detection of time anomalies specifically, as well as for the detection of objects that move rapidly in space, near earth objects, asteroids, meteors, et cetera. Those could be leveraged for the study of UAPs."* The astrophysics decadal survey recommended agencies (NSF, DOE, NASA) work together, share data, and share facilities.
- **Limitations:** Pointed at space, not Earth's atmosphere. Would need retasking or recalibration for atmospheric UAP detection. Federica noted facilities specifically designed to detect UAPs are under development by other groups.
- **Referenced by:** Federica Bianco, David Grinspoon

### 25. Geographical/Geophysical Observatories

- **Owner/Operator:** Various scientific institutions
- **Description:** Ground-based instruments for monitoring Earth's geophysical properties.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Federica Bianco mentioned these alongside astrophysical observatories as existing infrastructure that could potentially be leveraged for multi-sensor, multi-platform, multi-site data collection for UAP studies.
- **Limitations:** Not designed for aerial anomaly detection. Would require evaluation of whether these instruments can detect UAP signatures given the known UAP target parameters.
- **Referenced by:** Federica Bianco

### 26. Gravitational Wave Detectors

- **Owner/Operator:** NASA / Scientific Community
- **Description:** Instruments for detecting gravitational waves — ripples in spacetime caused by massive cosmic events.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Kirkpatrick listed gravitational wave detection among NASA's unique capabilities that could potentially assist UAP efforts: *"NASA also has access to... tools for gravitational wave, geomagnetic wave detection."* Mentioned as part of the broad spectrum of scientific instruments that could be evaluated for UAP relevance.
- **Limitations:** Not designed for UAP detection. Extremely specialized instruments for specific physical phenomena. Unclear whether UAP would produce detectable gravitational wave signatures.
- **Referenced by:** Sean Kirkpatrick

### 27. Geomagnetic Wave Detectors

- **Owner/Operator:** NASA / Scientific Community
- **Description:** Instruments for measuring variations in Earth's magnetic field.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Kirkpatrick listed geomagnetic wave detection alongside gravitational wave tools as NASA capabilities that *"may assist both Aero and NASA in their UAP efforts."* If UAP produce electromagnetic or geomagnetic disturbances, these detectors could potentially capture signatures.
- **Limitations:** Not designed for UAP detection. Would need evaluation against the UAP target package to determine relevance.
- **Referenced by:** Sean Kirkpatrick

### 28. Radiological Sensors

- **Owner/Operator:** NASA / Scientific Community
- **Description:** Sensors for detecting and measuring radiation.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Kirkpatrick listed radiological sensors among NASA's capabilities: *"NASA also has access to... radiological sensors."* If UAP emit unusual radiation signatures, these sensors could potentially detect them.
- **Limitations:** Not designed or deployed for UAP detection. Would need to be evaluated for relevance to UAP phenomenology.
- **Referenced by:** Sean Kirkpatrick

### 29. National Weather Service Weather Balloons

- **Owner/Operator:** National Weather Service (NOAA)
- **Description:** 92 weather stations release balloons twice daily (0000Z and 1200Z). Balloons fly up to 100,000 feet, typically lasting ~2 hours before the envelope bursts. At least 184 balloon flights daily in the U.S. (~1,600 worldwide). Tracked and positions provided to air traffic control.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Weather balloons are a **major source of UAP misidentification**. Mike Freie provided detailed data on balloon launches. Scott Kelly recounted mistaking a Bart Simpson balloon for a UFO. Karlin Toner noted the Chinese balloon incident caused a significant uptick in UAP reports (68 reports starting in February 2013). Balloons are part of the "background" that must be characterized to identify true anomalies.
- **Limitations:** Balloon tracking data exists but may not be fully integrated into UAP investigation workflows. Commercial and hobbyist balloons also launched but may not be as well tracked. Balloons can appear anomalous to observers unfamiliar with their behavior at altitude.
- **Referenced by:** Mike Freie, Scott Kelly, Karlin Toner, David Spergel, Nadia Drake

## Crowdsource/Public Data

### 30. iPhone/Cell Phone Sensors

- **Owner/Operator:** Public / Citizens
- **Description:** Smartphone cameras, GPS, accelerometers, magnetometers, and other sensors. ~3-4 billion smartphones globally.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Kirkpatrick noted: *"No, imagery from a iPhone is generally not helpful unless you are right up on whatever it is you're looking at. However, some of the ancillary data that your iPhone provides from location to speed to other phenomenologies and more than one of those can be very helpful."* David Spergel was enthusiastic: *"I remain a big fan of these things... they are fabulous data collectors... NASA I think has the prestige and visibility to develop an app or work with companies to develop apps that could collect data in a uniform and centralized way."* Federica Bianco discussed cell phones being used for crowdsourcing in astrophysics and space science.
- **Limitations:** Kirkpatrick was direct: iPhone imagery alone is "generally not helpful." Cameras are uncalibrated, lack metadata about sensor characteristics, and suffer from optical artifacts. Walter Scott asked about "open sourcing a set of applications for cell phones to be able to pull in a lot of the right metadata." Federica emphasized that a platform is needed — not just guidelines — to ensure proper metadata collection, data transmission, and community coordination for follow-up observations.
- **Referenced by:** Sean Kirkpatrick, David Spergel, Federica Bianco, Walter Scott

### 31. Citizen Science Reports / Proposed Crowdsourcing Platform

- **Owner/Operator:** Public (proposed NASA-facilitated platform)
- **Description:** A proposed centralized platform for the public to report UAP sightings with standardized data and metadata collection, including sensor information, timestamps, geolocation, and environmental conditions.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Multiple panelists advocated for this. Federica Bianco: *"We could crowdsource the data collection if a platform to crowdsource exists that supports the collection of data and metadata, and the transmission of data and metadata. We think that NASA might be able to play an important role in the development of this platform."* David Spergel saw it as an opportunity for citizen science with multi-camera observations to eliminate optical limitations. Karlin Toner recommended NASA "assess the possibility of crowdsourcing data."
- **Limitations:** Current UAP data from the public is uncalibrated, poorly documented, and incomplete. Federica noted the data doesn't meet FAIR standards (findability, accessibility, interoperability, reusability). A platform would need to reach a large enough community, ensure proper metadata collection, and enable coordinated follow-up observations. Synthetic data generation for training AI models was discussed but noted as risky due to embedded biases.
- **Referenced by:** Federica Bianco, David Spergel, Karlin Toner, Walter Scott, Jennifer Buss

### 32. Social Media Data

- **Owner/Operator:** Public / Social Media Platforms
- **Description:** Public posts, images, videos, and discussions about UAP sightings on social media platforms.
- **Relevance to UAP:** An unnamed panelist asked: *"Can we use social media, that kind of thing as a way of queuing to know where something's happening? 'Cause we know Google surges can lead you to better understand where outbreaks happen, disease outbreaks. Can you use similar type of conflation of data to start saying, well something's going on here, let's start queuing sensors in that area."* Proposed as a tip-and-cue mechanism for directing scientific sensors toward active UAP areas.
- **Limitations:** Social media data is unverified, uncalibrated, and subject to manipulation, hoaxes, and misidentification. Quality control would be extremely challenging. The analogy to Google search surges for disease outbreaks was noted but not validated for UAP applications.
- **Referenced by:** Speaker 4 (unnamed panelist)

### 33. Eyewitness Reports (Pilots, Military, Public)

- **Owner/Operator:** Various (military aviators, commercial pilots, air traffic controllers, general public)
- **Description:** First-person accounts of UAP sightings from credible witnesses including trained military and commercial pilots, air traffic controllers, and the general public.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Eyewitness reports form the foundation of most UAP data. Nadia Drake noted: *"Recently, many credible witnesses have reported seeing unidentified objects in the sky, some of which are behaving rather peculiarly. In some instances, these reports include corroborating data from various instruments."* Federica Bianco elevated their importance: *"Eyewitnesses reports... cannot ascertain the nature of UAPs. However, they should be considered because they may contain important information, for example, persistent sighting locations or seasonality."*
- **Limitations:** Universally acknowledged as insufficient on their own. Nicola Fox: *"The existing data available from eyewitness reports are often muddled and cannot provide conclusive evidence."* David Spergel: *"Current existing data and eyewitness reports alone are insufficient to provide conclusive evidence about the nature and origin of every UAP event."* Federica: Eyewitness reports need to be *"joined with quantitative data collected by sensors as well as biophysical and psychophysical assessments of the witness."* Scott Kelly emphasized the environment is *"very, very conducive to optical illusions."* Stigma significantly impairs reporting — commercial pilots are "very reluctant to report anomalies" (Spergel).
- **Referenced by:** Nadia Drake, Nicola Fox, David Spergel, Federica Bianco, Scott Kelly, Karlin Toner, Mike Freie

### 34. Google Search Surge Data (Analogy)

- **Owner/Operator:** Google / Public
- **Description:** Aggregated search query data that can indicate emerging events or trends (e.g., disease outbreak detection through search pattern analysis).
- **Relevance to UAP:** Proposed as an analogy for using public behavior data as a tip-and-cue mechanism for UAP detection. An unnamed panelist suggested: *"Can we use social media... we know Google surges can lead you to better understand where outbreaks happen... Can you use similar type of conflation of data to start saying, well something's going on here."*
- **Limitations:** Referenced only as an analogy, not as a concrete proposal. The applicability of search surge methodology to UAP detection was not validated.
- **Referenced by:** Speaker 4 (unnamed panelist)

## Databases/Archives

### 35. AARO UAP Database (~800+ Cases)

- **Owner/Operator:** AARO (All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office, Department of Defense)
- **Description:** The primary U.S. government database of UAP reports. At the time of the meeting, contained over 800 cases collected over approximately 27 years. Growing at 50-100 new reports per month. Less than single-digit percentages (estimated 2-5%) display potentially anomalous characteristics.
- **Relevance to UAP:** This is the central repository for UAP data in the U.S. government. Kirkpatrick presented analytic trends from this database including altitude distributions, morphologies (orbs/spheres being most common at ~half of cases), and geographic heat maps. The database includes both classified and unclassified reports from military aviators, sensors, and recently integrated FAA data.
- **Limitations:** Most cases lack sufficient data for resolution. Kirkpatrick: *"While a large number of cases in Aero's holdings remain technically unresolved, this is primarily due to a lack of data associated with those cases."* Collection is biased toward areas where military sensors and operators are active. The database is largely classified, limiting open scientific analysis. Historical data is fragmented and inconsistently collected.
- **Referenced by:** Sean Kirkpatrick, Nadia Drake, Karlin Toner

### 36. NASA Open Data Portal (data.nasa.gov)

- **Owner/Operator:** NASA
- **Description:** NASA's public data portal providing free access to tens of thousands of datasets from NASA missions and research programs.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Nicola Fox explicitly invited the public to use this resource: *"I invite you to visit our open data portal at data.nasa.gov to comb through our tens of thousands of data sets that are free and fully accessible to the public."* Paula Bontempi noted NASA's "tremendous archive of data from satellites and other space-based and ground networks." Federica Bianco highlighted NASA's experience in data curation and its open data policy as assets for UAP research.
- **Limitations:** Data was not collected for UAP detection purposes. Jennifer Buss noted: *"The data that we recognized were not collected for the purpose of identifying UAP, which leaves bias in the data that was collected."* Sensors were not calibrated for identifying anomalous phenomena. Data may be hard to access and requires expertise to process.
- **Referenced by:** Nicola Fox, Paula Bontempi, Federica Bianco, Jennifer Buss

### 37. data.gov

- **Owner/Operator:** U.S. Government
- **Description:** The U.S. government's central open data portal providing access to federal datasets across all agencies.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Nicola Fox recommended: *"Please check out data.gov/open/data for a great overview of where you can find the archives for our science and mission data sites."* Serves as a gateway to cross-agency data that could be relevant for UAP research.
- **Limitations:** Same as NASA's data portal — data not collected for UAP purposes. Vast quantity of data makes targeted searching challenging without specific parameters.
- **Referenced by:** Nicola Fox

### 38. FAA Domestic Event Network

- **Owner/Operator:** Federal Aviation Administration
- **Description:** FAA's internal reporting system for unusual events, including UAP sightings reported by air traffic controllers.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Mike Freie identified this as the destination for controller UAP reports: *"They're reported to the domestic event network, it's an FAA organization."* Historically received 3-5 reports per month from controllers nationwide, with upticks during notable events (Starlink launches in August 2022: 8-10 reports; Chinese balloon incident in February: 68 reports).
- **Limitations:** Mike Gold asked whether this data was archived and shared with AARO; Mike Freie said he believed it was archived and presumed it would feed into AARO's database but could not confirm. Reporting is limited to air traffic controllers; the process for pilots to report was unclear. Low reporting rate may reflect stigma or lack of awareness.
- **Referenced by:** Mike Freie, Mike Gold

### 39. NASA Archival Satellite & Mission Data

- **Owner/Operator:** NASA
- **Description:** Decades of archived data from NASA's satellite missions, ground networks, and other observational assets. Includes Earth science, atmospheric, and space observation data.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Sean Kirkpatrick recommended: *"Archived scientific data. We have a whole bunch of calibrated large scale scientific data from all these different instrumentations around the world. Taking a look at how can you apply maybe some AI/ML tools to search through that data for anomalous signatures that may correlate to things that we've got reporting on."* Paula Bontempi emphasized the archive's value. Federica Bianco noted NASA has "spearheaded an effort to review NASA and NASA partner archival data to prepare them for machine learning and AI."
- **Limitations:** Data not collected for UAP detection. Applying AI/ML to archival data is challenging because current UAP data doesn't meet the standards needed for training models. Federica noted: *"The current status of the UAP data will make this lift really hard compared to even what is being done by NASA for astrophysical data."*
- **Referenced by:** Sean Kirkpatrick, Paula Bontempi, Federica Bianco

### 40. Archived Scientific Data (Global Instruments)

- **Owner/Operator:** Various scientific institutions worldwide
- **Description:** Calibrated, large-scale scientific data from instruments and observatories around the world, including astrophysical, geophysical, and atmospheric measurements.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Kirkpatrick recommended searching archived scientific data with AI/ML tools for anomalous signatures correlating with UAP reports. Federica Bianco discussed how astrophysical observatories designed for time-domain anomaly detection could be leveraged. The concept is to mine existing high-quality scientific data for events that match UAP characteristics.
- **Limitations:** Data was collected for other scientific purposes. Searching requires knowing what signatures to look for. AI/ML application is limited by the lack of well-characterized UAP data for training. The "needle in a haystack" problem is compounded across multiple heterogeneous datasets.
- **Referenced by:** Sean Kirkpatrick, Federica Bianco

### 41. ODNI UAP Report (Predecessor Database, ~500 Cases)

- **Owner/Operator:** Office of the Director of National Intelligence
- **Description:** The preliminary ODNI report on UAP that contained approximately 500 cases, serving as the predecessor to AARO's expanded database.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Karlin Toner referenced the growth from the ODNI report: *"Growing from an ODNI report that was something like 500 to I think this morning we heard something like 800 now. That's accelerating."* Demonstrates the rapid growth in UAP reporting as stigma decreases and reporting mechanisms improve.
- **Limitations:** Superseded by AARO's database. Same limitations as the AARO database regarding data quality and resolution rates.
- **Referenced by:** Karlin Toner

### 42. SETI Radio/Optical Signal Data

- **Owner/Operator:** SETI (Search for Extraterrestrial Intelligence) Community
- **Description:** Decades of radio and optical signal data collected by SETI programs searching for evidence of extraterrestrial technological activity.
- **Relevance to UAP:** David Grinspoon drew parallels between SETI methodology and UAP research: *"The SETI community has expertise in methodologies for determining whether a potential techno signature is in fact a natural phenomenon or misinterpreted terrestrial technology."* SETI's approach of asking "Is it a real signal? Is it a known or unknown natural phenomena? Must it be technological? Is it known terrestrial technology?" is directly applicable to UAP analysis. An unnamed panelist noted SETI has "very well standardized data sets" that have been collected for some time, enabling AI/ML application — a model the UAP field could learn from.
- **Limitations:** SETI data is focused on signals from space, not atmospheric phenomena. Grinspoon noted that the broader "techno signatures" field (beyond classical SETI signal searching) has not been collecting data for as long. The stigma that once surrounded SETI/techno signatures is analogous to the current UAP stigma.
- **Referenced by:** David Grinspoon, Shelley Wright, Speaker 5 (unnamed panelist)

## Key Cross-Cutting Themes

### Data Quality and Calibration

The most consistent theme across all speakers was the critical need for **high-quality, well-calibrated data**:

- **David Spergel:** *"The lesson of my career has been you want to address important questions, you need high quality data with well calibrated instruments."*
- **Nicola Fox:** *"Right now, there is a very limited number of high quality observations and data curation of UAP."*
- **Federica Bianco:** *"The current status of the data about UAPs does not meet [FAIR] standards. The data collection is inconsistent. It's inhomogenous, it's uncalibrated."*
- **Sean Kirkpatrick:** *"Without sufficient data, we are unable to reach defendable conclusions that meet the high scientific standards we set for resolution."*

### The Sensor Calibration Problem

A fundamental challenge identified throughout the meeting is that **existing sensors were not designed for UAP detection**:

- DOD sensors are designed for targeting known threats, not characterizing anomalies
- FAA radars are optimized for manned aircraft and filter out small/slow objects
- NASA Earth science satellites are designed for environmental monitoring, not fast-moving aerial objects
- Citizen smartphone cameras lack calibration and metadata

### The "Needle in a Haystack" Problem

Multiple speakers described UAP detection as finding a needle in a haystack — or more precisely, finding an anomaly in a haystack of known objects:

- 45,000 daily flights in U.S. airspace (FAA)
- 880,000 registered commercial drones + 1.69 million recreational drones
- 184+ weather balloon launches daily in the U.S. (~1,600 worldwide)
- Commercial satellites, military equipment, ionospheric phenomena, and other background sources

### The Need for Multi-Sensor, Multi-Platform Approaches

Josh Sumeta's analysis of the "Go Fast" video demonstrated the critical importance of multi-sensor data:

- The Navy aviator footage included ranging data, infrared imaging, elevation angle, azimuth angle, target range, aircraft altitude, time reference, and air speed
- This allowed quantitative reconstruction showing the object was moving at ~40 mph (consistent with wind speeds at 13,000 feet), not the dramatic speed it appeared
- Without this contextual data, the optical illusion would have remained unresolved

### Tip-and-Cue Systems

Sean Kirkpatrick and others discussed the need for real-time tip-and-cue systems:

- When a UAP is reported, multiple sensors (ground-based, space-based, scientific, academic) should be rapidly directed to the location
- Social media and citizen reports could serve as initial tips
- This requires pre-established protocols and sensor network coordination
