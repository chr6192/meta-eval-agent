# Data Sources and Measurement Systems Referenced in NASA UAP Public Meeting

This document catalogs all data sources, sensors, databases, and measurement systems referenced during NASA's first public meeting on Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena (UAPs), held to inform the NASA UAP Independent Study Team's recommendations.

## 1. Government/Military Sensors

### DOD/IC Sensors
- **Owner/Operator:** Department of Defense (DOD) / Intelligence Community (IC)
- **Description:** Broad range of military and intelligence sensors including radar, electro-optical (EO), infrared (IR), and signals intelligence (SIGINT) systems used for national security surveillance.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Primary source of UAP reports to AARO. Sean Kirkpatrick noted that DOD sensors are designed to identify known objects and put weapons on them, not for scientific data collection. IC sensors, by contrast, are described as "very close to scientific sensors" — calibrated, high-precision, and well-characterized.
- **Limitations:** DOD sensors are not scientific sensors; they are not calibrated for scientific data collection. Data is often classified due to sensor platform sensitivity, not because of the UAP subject itself. Most sensors are pointed around the continental U.S. and cannot be arbitrarily redirected without legal constraints.
- **Referenced by:** Sean Kirkpatrick (AARO)

### MQ-9 Reaper EO Sensor
- **Owner/Operator:** Department of Defense (Air Force)
- **Description:** Electro-optical sensor mounted on MQ-9 Reaper unmanned aerial vehicles, used for surveillance and reconnaissance.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Used to capture the "metallic orb" UAP footage shown during the meeting — a spherical object observed in the Middle East in 2022. Demonstrated as a typical example of objects seen in UAP reports.
- **Limitations:** Sensor jitter and platform motion can create apparent movement of objects. Without additional data (range, multi-sensor confirmation), size and speed assessments are unreliable.
- **Referenced by:** Sean Kirkpatrick (AARO)

### F-35 Sensor Suite
- **Owner/Operator:** Department of Defense / Lockheed Martin
- **Description:** Advanced sensor suite on the F-35 Lightning II fighter jet, including AESA radar, Distributed Aperture System (DAS), and electro-optical targeting system (EOTS).
- **Relevance to UAP:** Referenced as an example of how classified sensor platforms classify even mundane imagery (e.g., "if a camera on an F-35 took a picture of a bird, it's classified"). AARO is conducting calibration flights of F-35s against known objects like weather balloons.
- **Limitations:** Data is classified due to sensor capabilities. Sensors are designed for combat identification, not scientific observation.
- **Referenced by:** David Spergel, Sean Kirkpatrick

### P-3 Orion Sensors
- **Owner/Operator:** Department of Defense (Navy)
- **Description:** Maritime patrol aircraft with various sensor systems including radar, EO, and magnetic anomaly detection.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Used in the newly released video showing three dots that were initially reported as UAP but later identified as commercial aircraft on a major flight corridor. Demonstrates how sensor and platform motion can create misperceptions.
- **Limitations:** Sensor jitter and parallax effects can mislead operators about object distance, speed, and behavior. Objects appeared closer than they were.
- **Referenced by:** Sean Kirkpatrick (AARO)

### NOAA Sensors
- **Owner/Operator:** National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA)
- **Description:** Various environmental monitoring sensors including weather satellites, oceanographic sensors, and atmospheric monitoring systems.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Referenced by AARO as part of the interagency partnership for UAP data collection and analysis. Can contribute to understanding environmental conditions during UAP events.
- **Limitations:** Not designed for UAP detection; primarily focused on weather and climate monitoring.
- **Referenced by:** Sean Kirkpatrick (AARO)

## 2. Civilian Aviation Systems

### FAA Short-Range Radar Systems (ASR)
- **Owner/Operator:** Federal Aviation Administration (FAA)
- **Description:** Airport surveillance radars located at high and medium density airports. Detection range: 40-60 miles; altitude detection: up to 24,000 feet. Fixed-face systems rotating at 12 RPM (terminal) or 5 RPM (en route).
- **Relevance to UAP:** Primary civilian airspace surveillance system. Can detect non-cooperative targets. Data retention exists for legal/accident investigation purposes.
- **Limitations:** Detection depends on target size and altitude. Small UAS (0.01 m²) are much harder to detect than large airliners (100 m²). Coverage gaps exist, especially in the western U.S. due to terrain. Systems are filtered to remove biologicals, insects, dust, and ground clutter — which means they are optimized for manned aircraft, not anomalous phenomena.
- **Referenced by:** Mike Freie (FAA)

### FAA Long-Range Radar Systems (ARSR-4, CRSR)
- **Owner/Operator:** Federal Aviation Administration (FAA)
- **Description:** Air route surveillance radars. ARSR-4s located around U.S. perimeter; CRSR systems located in the interior. Detection range: 200-250 nautical miles; altitude detection: up to 100,000 feet.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Provide broader coverage for en-route air traffic and national defense. ARSR-4s support homeland security missions.
- **Limitations:** Same as short-range radars regarding target size dependency and filtering. Line-of-sight limitations due to Earth's curvature and terrain screening in mountainous areas.
- **Referenced by:** Mike Freie (FAA)

### ADS-B (Automatic Dependent Surveillance-Broadcast)
- **Owner/Operator:** Federal Aviation Administration (FAA)
- **Description:** Cooperative surveillance system where aircraft broadcast their GPS position, altitude, velocity, and other data. Primary data source for modern airspace management.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Provides excellent coverage of cooperative aircraft down to 1,500 feet AGL. Can help identify known aircraft that might be mistaken for UAP.
- **Limitations:** Only tracks cooperative aircraft equipped with ADS-B transponders. Does not detect non-cooperative targets, drones, balloons, or other objects without transponders.
- **Referenced by:** Mike Freie (FAA)

### FAA UAP Reporting System (Air Traffic Controller Reports)
- **Owner/Operator:** Federal Aviation Administration (FAA)
- **Description:** Process by which air traffic controllers can report UAP sightings or events, defined in the air traffic controller order.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Historically receives 3-5 reports per month across all 14,000 controllers. Saw an uptick to 8-10 in August 2022 (possibly due to Starlink launches) and 68 reports starting in February 2023 (following the Chinese balloon incident).
- **Limitations:** Very low reporting rate relative to air traffic volume. Reports are archived but process is not well-publicized for pilots.
- **Referenced by:** Mike Freie (FAA), Mike Gold

### Air Traffic Control (ATC) Data / Domestic Event Network (DEN)
- **Owner/Operator:** Federal Aviation Administration (FAA)
- **Description:** Processed radar data displayed on controller screens (ERAM and STAR systems). Long-term retention exists for legal/accident investigation purposes, measured in months.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Can be used to cross-reference UAP reports with known air traffic. AARO has integrated FAA data into its database.
- **Limitations:** Raw radar data (INQ data) is not retained due to huge volume (gigabytes). Only processed/displayed data is kept. Data has undergone filtering to optimize controller displays, removing non-aircraft returns.
- **Referenced by:** Mike Freie (FAA), Walter, Mike Gold

### National Weather Service Balloon Data
- **Owner/Operator:** National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA)
- **Description:** Weather balloons launched from 92 weather stations twice daily (00Z and 12Z), flying up to 100,000 feet with tracking data provided to ATC.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Represents a significant source of aerial objects that can be mistaken for UAP. At least 184 balloon flights daily in the U.S., plus university and hobbyist launches.
- **Limitations:** Balloons are tracked and their positions known to ATC, but they can still appear as anomalies in other sensors. Small size makes them difficult to detect on radar.
- **Referenced by:** Mike Freie (FAA), Nadia Drake

## 3. Space-Based Assets

### NASA Earth Sensing Satellites
- **Owner/Operator:** NASA
- **Description:** Multiple satellite missions collecting data on Earth's atmosphere, oceans, land, and climate. Includes optical, infrared, microwave, and radar sensors.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Can potentially detect atmospheric anomalies, unusual objects, or environmental conditions. AARO is evaluating whether earth sensing satellites can detect typical UAP characteristics.
- **Limitations:** Not designed for UAP detection. Frame rates may be too slow for fast-moving objects. Spatial resolution may not be sufficient for small (1-4 meter) objects.
- **Referenced by:** Sean Kirkpatrick (AARO), Shelley Wright

### Hubble Space Telescope
- **Owner/Operator:** NASA / European Space Agency (ESA) / Space Telescope Science Institute (STScI)
- **Description:** Space-based optical telescope in low Earth orbit, observing in ultraviolet, visible, and near-infrared wavelengths.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Referenced as an example of well-calibrated scientific instruments. Used to illustrate optical phenomena (ghosting, detector saturation, diffraction spikes) that can create anomalous images.
- **Limitations:** Not designed for Earth or near-Earth observation. Pointed away from Earth. Optical artifacts can create misleading images.
- **Referenced by:** David Spergel

### James Webb Space Telescope (JWST)
- **Owner/Operator:** NASA / ESA / Canadian Space Agency (CSA)
- **Description:** Infrared space telescope operating at L2 Lagrange point.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Referenced as an example of well-calibrated scientific instruments. Used to illustrate detector saturation, bleeding, and optical artifacts that can create strange images.
- **Limitations:** Not designed for Earth observation. Optical artifacts (diffraction spikes, saturation, ghosting) can produce misleading images.
- **Referenced by:** David Spergel

### Commercial Satellite Data
- **Owner/Operator:** Commercial space companies
- **Description:** Satellite imagery and data from commercial providers.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Some companies lobbied to present to the panel. Have "troves of data" that could potentially be searched for anomalous signatures.
- **Limitations:** Data collected for various purposes other than UAP detection. While well-calibrated, represents only one source among many needed.
- **Referenced by:** Jennifer Buss (panel)

### High-Speed Imaging from Space Station
- **Owner/Operator:** NASA / International Partners
- **Description:** High-speed cameras and imaging systems on the International Space Station.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Used to capture ionospheric phenomena like sprites (upward-going lightning) that were initially not believed when reported by pilots.
- **Limitations:** Limited field of view. Primarily used for specific scientific objectives, not UAP surveillance.
- **Referenced by:** David Spergel

## 4. Ground-Based Scientific Instruments

### Radiological Sensors
- **Owner/Operator:** Various (government, academic, international)
- **Description:** Sensors detecting radioactive emissions, gamma rays, and other radiological signatures.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Referenced by AARO as one of the types of sensors that could potentially detect anomalous signatures from UAP.
- **Limitations:** Not specifically designed for UAP detection. Would require calibration and modeling to understand what signatures to expect.
- **Referenced by:** Sean Kirkpatrick (AARO)

### Gravitational Wave Detection Tools
- **Owner/Operator:** LIGO, Virgo, KAGRA collaborations
- **Description:** Ground-based interferometers detecting gravitational waves from cosmic events.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Referenced by AARO as an example of sophisticated scientific instrumentation that NASA has access to or partnerships with.
- **Limitations:** Not designed for UAP detection. Detects cosmic-scale events, not atmospheric phenomena.
- **Referenced by:** Sean Kirkpatrick (AARO)

### Geomagnetic Wave Detection Tools
- **Owner/Operator:** Various (geophysical observatories, space weather monitoring stations)
- **Description:** Instruments measuring variations in Earth's magnetic field.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Referenced by AARO as potentially relevant for detecting anomalous signatures.
- **Limitations:** Not designed for UAP detection. Would require significant modeling to understand expected signatures.
- **Referenced by:** Sean Kirkpatrick (AARO)

### Fast Radio Burst (FRB) Observatory Data
- **Owner/Operator:** Various (Parkes Observatory in Australia, CHIME, etc.)
- **Description:** Radio telescopes detecting fast radio bursts — brief, intense pulses of radio emission from distant galaxies.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Used as an analogy for how sensitive scientific instruments can detect unexpected phenomena. The "microwave oven" story illustrated how mundane sources can create anomalous signals in sensitive detectors.
- **Limitations:** Detects cosmic radio signals, not atmospheric objects. The analogy is about methodology, not direct UAP detection.
- **Referenced by:** David Spergel

### Large-Scale Ground-Based Scientific Instrumentation
- **Owner/Operator:** Various (academic institutions, national laboratories, international collaborations)
- **Description:** Large scientific facilities including radio telescopes, optical observatories, particle detectors, and atmospheric monitoring stations.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Sean Kirkpatrick recommended evaluating how these instruments could be used for UAP detection, given a "surrogate target package" of expected UAP characteristics. Could provide "tipping and queuing" for other sensors.
- **Limitations:** Not designed for UAP detection. Would require modeling and simulation to determine if they could detect expected UAP signatures. Need for calibration against known objects.
- **Referenced by:** Sean Kirkpatrick (AARO)

## 5. Crowdsource/Public Data

### iPhone / Smartphone Sensors
- **Owner/Operator:** Public / Commercial (Apple, Samsung, etc.)
- **Description:** Built-in sensors in smartphones including cameras, GPS, accelerometers, gyroscopes, and magnetometers.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Sean Kirkpatrick noted that while iPhone imagery is "generally not helpful unless you are right up on whatever it is," the ancillary data (location, speed, other phenomenologies) from multiple phones can be very helpful for triangulation and corroboration.
- **Limitations:** Camera quality insufficient for distant objects. Lack of calibration. Metadata may be incomplete or inaccurate. Single-sensor observations are limited.
- **Referenced by:** Sean Kirkpatrick (AARO)

### Crowdsourcing Platforms
- **Owner/Operator:** Various (nonprofit, for-profit, government)
- **Description:** Platforms for collecting public reports, images, and data about UAP sightings.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Federica Bianco discussed the potential for a crowdsourcing platform that could collect standardized data and metadata from the public, enabling follow-up observations by professional instruments when something is sighted.
- **Limitations:** Current crowdsourced data is uncalibrated, poorly documented, and incomplete. Eyewitness reports alone cannot ascertain the nature of UAPs. Quality control is a significant challenge.
- **Referenced by:** Federica Bianco, Sean Kirkpatrick (AARO), Walter

## 6. Databases/Archives

### AARO Database
- **Owner/Operator:** All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO), Department of Defense
- **Description:** Centralized database of UAP reports from military, intelligence community, and (recently) FAA sources. Contains over 800 cases as of the meeting date.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Primary government repository for UAP data. AARO uses it for trend analysis, case resolution, and reporting to Congress.
- **Limitations:** Only a small percentage (2-5%) display potentially anomalous characteristics. Most cases lack sufficient data for definitive resolution. Many remain unresolved due to data gaps.
- **Referenced by:** Sean Kirkpatrick (AARO), Nadia Drake

### NASA Open Data Portal (data.nasa.gov)
- **Owner/Operator:** NASA
- **Description:** Public repository of tens of thousands of NASA datasets, free and fully accessible.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Dr. Fox invited the public to explore NASA's open data for scientific research. Could be searched for anomalous signatures or environmental conditions during reported UAP events.
- **Limitations:** Data was not collected for UAP detection. Requires expertise to interpret. Not all data is well-calibrated or easily searchable for UAP-related anomalies.
- **Referenced by:** Nicola Fox (NASA)

### data.gov
- **Owner/Operator:** U.S. General Services Administration
- **Description:** Federal open data portal providing access to datasets from across the U.S. government.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Provides overview of where to find science and mission data archives from various agencies.
- **Limitations:** Aggregated portal; specific UAP-relevant data requires targeted searching.
- **Referenced by:** Nicola Fox (NASA)

### Domestic Event Network (DEN)
- **Owner/Operator:** Federal Aviation Administration (FAA)
- **Description:** FAA organization/function where air traffic controllers report UAP sightings.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Repository for controller-reported UAP events. Data is archived and presumably fed into AARO's database.
- **Limitations:** Very low reporting rate (3-5 per month historically). Process is not well-known to pilots.
- **Referenced by:** Mike Freie (FAA), Mike Gold

### Five Eyes Intelligence Sharing
- **Owner/Operator:** UK, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, United States
- **Description:** Intelligence alliance for data sharing and collaboration on security matters, now extended to UAP topics.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Sean Kirkpatrick held the first Five Eyes forum on UAP, discussing data sharing, reporting methods, analysis collaboration, and calibration assistance.
- **Limitations:** Primarily focused on national security aspects. Scientific and academic relationships need expansion.
- **Referenced by:** Sean Kirkpatrick (AARO)

### SETI Data Archives
- **Owner/Operator:** SETI Institute / Various academic institutions
- **Description:** Archives of radio and optical data from the Search for Extraterrestrial Intelligence.
- **Relevance to UAP:** David Grinspoon discussed SETI's expertise in identifying anomalous signatures and determining whether they have mundane explanations. The methodologies are relevant to UAP analysis.
- **Limitations:** Focused on cosmic signals, not atmospheric phenomena. However, the analytical frameworks are transferable.
- **Referenced by:** David Grinspoon

### NASA Astrobiology Data
- **Owner/Operator:** NASA
- **Description:** Data from NASA's astrobiology research, including studies of biosignatures and technosignatures.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Provides frameworks for detecting anomalous signatures and determining their origins. NASA's planetary exploration data could be searched for anomalous objects in the solar system.
- **Limitations:** Not designed for UAP detection. Focused on distant cosmic and planetary phenomena.
- **Referenced by:** David Grinspoon

### Archived Scientific Data
- **Owner/Operator:** Various (NASA, NOAA, academic institutions, international partners)
- **Description:** Calibrated large-scale scientific data from instrumentation around the world.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Sean Kirkpatrick recommended applying AI/ML tools to search through archived scientific data for anomalous signatures that might correlate to UAP reports.
- **Limitations:** Vast volume of data. Requires knowing what signatures to search for. Need for cross-calibration between different instruments and datasets.
- **Referenced by:** Sean Kirkpatrick (AARO)

## Additional Systems and Concepts Referenced

### AI/ML Analytic Techniques
- **Description:** Artificial intelligence and machine learning methods for anomaly detection, natural language processing (for pilot reports), and target recognition.
- **Relevance to UAP:** AARO is researching how to apply AI/ML to UAP data, including training models to identify known objects (like balloons) and detect anomalies in large datasets.
- **Limitations:** Cannot be applied effectively until data meets FAIR standards (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable). Current UAP data is inconsistent, uncalibrated, and poorly documented.
- **Referenced by:** Sean Kirkpatrick (AARO), Anamaria Berea, Federica Bianco

### Dedicated Purpose-Built UAP Sensors
- **Owner/Operator:** AARO / In development
- **Description:** Sensors specifically designed to detect, track, and characterize UAP based on observed characteristics (size, altitude, speed, signature).
- **Relevance to UAP:** AARO is developing these as a bootstrap method — broad spectrum search across few indicators, then refining as more data is collected.
- **Limitations:** Still in early development. Need modeling and simulation to validate detection capabilities. Legal restrictions on where sensors can be pointed (especially over U.S. territory).
- **Referenced by:** Sean Kirkpatrick (AARO)

### Multi-Sensor, Multi-Platform, Multi-Site Data Collection
- **Description:** Concept for collecting UAP data simultaneously from different platforms (ground, air, space) and locations.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Josh emphasized that the Navy aviator cases with the most detailed information used a combination of ranging and infrared imaging. Multi-sensor approach is considered critical for UAP investigations.
- **Limitations:** Requires coordination across multiple organizations and systems. Data synchronization and calibration are significant challenges.
- **Referenced by:** Josh, Federica Bianco, Shelley Wright
