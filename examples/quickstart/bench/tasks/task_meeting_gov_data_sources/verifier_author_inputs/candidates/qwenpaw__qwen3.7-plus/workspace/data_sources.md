## 1. Government/Military Sensors

### 1.1 DOD Military Sensors (General)
- **Owner/Operator:** Department of Defense
- **Description:** Broad category of sensor platforms mounted on military aircraft and platforms, designed primarily to identify known objects and target them with weapons. Includes electro-optical, infrared, and radar sensors across various military platforms.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Primary source of most UAP reports. AARO is evaluating these sensors against the typical UAP target package to determine their effectiveness for UAP detection. The vast majority of UAP reports come from DOD sensors.
- **Limitations:** Not scientific sensors; not calibrated for scientific data collection. Designed for one purpose: "identify an object that is known and put a weapon on it." Cannot be freely pointed at arbitrary locations due to legal restrictions. Single-purpose design means they may mischaracterize unknown objects.
- **Who referenced it:** Sean Kirkpatrick

### 1.2 Intelligence Community (IC) Sensors
- **Owner/Operator:** U.S. Intelligence Community
- **Description:** High-precision, calibrated sensor systems used for intelligence collection, including spy satellites and specialized reconnaissance platforms.
- **Relevance to UAP:** IC sensors are described as "very close to scientific sensors" — calibrated, high precision, and capable of detailed characterization. Data from these sensors on UAP events is typically classified due to the desire to protect technical capabilities.
- **Limitations:** Data is almost always classified. The classification is typically due to the sensor platform itself, not the UAP content. Cannot be openly shared for scientific analysis.
- **Who referenced it:** Sean Kirkpatrick, David Spergel

### 1.3 MQ-9 Electro-Optical (EO) Sensor
- **Owner/Operator:** U.S. Air Force / DOD
- **Description:** Electro-optical camera sensor mounted on the MQ-9 Reaper drone, used for surveillance and target identification. Captures visible and infrared imagery.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Used to capture footage of UAP events, including a well-known spherical orb sighting in the Middle East (2022). Provides ranging and imaging data that can be analyzed for object characteristics.
- **Limitations:** Sensor is mounted on a gimbal that may or may not be stabilized. Jitter from the platform can create apparent motion artifacts. Objects may appear to maneuver when the motion is actually from the sensor/platform. Distance estimation from single-sensor imagery is unreliable.
- **Who referenced it:** Sean Kirkpatrick

### 1.4 P-3 Aircraft Sensor Suite
- **Owner/Operator:** U.S. Navy / DOD
- **Description:** Sensor systems aboard P-3 maritime patrol aircraft, including radar and electro-optical sensors used during training missions.
- **Relevance to UAP:** A P-3 on a training mission in the western U.S. detected three objects that appeared anomalous. After analysis using air traffic control data, the objects were matched to commercial aircraft on a major flight corridor — demonstrating how sensor limitations can create false UAP reports.
- **Limitations:** Sensor defocusing and platform motion can create misleading impressions. Objects appeared closer than they actually were, leading to misperception of speed and behavior.
- **Who referenced it:** Sean Kirkpatrick

### 1.5 F-35 Camera/Sensor Suite
- **Owner/Operator:** U.S. Air Force / DOD
- **Description:** Advanced sensor and camera systems aboard the F-35 fighter jet, including electro-optical targeting systems.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Used as a key example of how military sensor data on UAP becomes classified — "If a camera on an F-35 took a picture of a bird, it's classified" because of the sensor capabilities, not the subject.
- **Limitations:** Data collected is classified due to the sensor platform's capabilities. Not available for open scientific analysis.
- **Who referenced it:** David Spergel, Sean Kirkpatrick

### 1.6 Navy Infrared Imaging & Ranging (ATFLIR)
- **Owner/Operator:** U.S. Navy
- **Description:** Advanced Targeting Forward-Looking Infrared system providing ranging data and infrared imaging from Navy aircraft. Used in combination with other sensors for multi-sensor observations.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Provides the most detailed UAP reports with contextual information. The multi-sensor approach (ranging + infrared) allows direct calculation of critical UAP parameters such as altitude and velocity. Used in analysis of the "Go Fast" video from USS Theodore Roosevelt (2015).
- **Limitations:** Range information can have uncertainties. Single-perspective observations are subject to parallax effects and optical illusions. Requires additional data (aircraft altitude, bearing, airspeed) for proper analysis.
- **Who referenced it:** Josh Sumeta

### 1.7 AARO Dedicated Purpose-Built Sensors
- **Owner/Operator:** AARO (All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office) / DOD
- **Description:** New sensors purpose-built and designed specifically to detect, track, and characterize UAP objects. Being deployed in select areas for surveillance. Designed based on the "typical UAP target package" derived from existing reports.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Represents AARO's effort to move beyond relying solely on existing DOD/IC sensors. Being calibrated against known objects (weather balloons, aircraft at various altitudes, speeds, sun conditions) to establish baselines.
- **Limitations:** Still in early deployment/bootstrap phase. Starting with broad spectrum search across few indicators. Legal restrictions limit where sensors can be pointed (especially over U.S. populated areas).
- **Who referenced it:** Sean Kirkpatrick

### 1.8 Five Eyes Data Sharing Network
- **Owner/Operator:** Five Eyes Alliance (United States, United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, New Zealand)
- **Description:** Intelligence and data sharing arrangement among the five partner nations for UAP-related information, including reporting data, analysis, and calibration support.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Recently established first Five Eyes forum on UAP. Partners will share data and information to feed into AARO's analytical process. Provides international calibration and analysis capabilities.
- **Limitations:** Still in early stages of establishing data-sharing protocols. Bound by each nation's classification and security requirements.
- **Who referenced it:** Sean Kirkpatrick

### 1.9 Joint Staff / Combatant Command Collection Systems
- **Owner/Operator:** DOD Joint Staff / Geographic Combatant Commands
- **Description:** The "largest collection apparatus in the entire globe" — referring to the totality of U.S. military intelligence collection capabilities that can be task-directed at specific locations.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Can be pointed at any location for UAP investigation when high confidence exists that something will be there. Used for tip-and-cue collection when pilots report sightings.
- **Limitations:** Cannot be pointed at arbitrary locations (especially over U.S. domestic areas) due to legal restrictions. Most collection is focused around CONUS and areas of military operations. Requires high confidence of target presence before tasking.
- **Who referenced it:** Sean Kirkpatrick

## 2. Civilian Aviation Systems

### 2.1 FAA Short-Range Radars (ASR — Airport Surveillance Radar)
- **Owner/Operator:** Federal Aviation Administration (FAA)
- **Description:** Radar systems located at high and medium density airports. Detection range of 40–60 miles with altitude detection capability of approximately 24,000 feet. Fixed-face rotating at 12 RPM (terminal) or 5 RPM.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Part of the non-cooperative surveillance layer that can detect unknown objects in terminal airspace. Provides data that AARO has integrated into its UAP database (contributing 100+ new cases).
- **Limitations:** Detection capability depends on target size (modeled on 1 sq meter target). Small drones (~0.01 sq m) are 100x harder to detect. Ground clutter filtering removes biologicals, insects, and debris — but may also filter anomalous objects. Coverage is denser in eastern U.S. due to airport density.
- **Who referenced it:** Mike Freie

### 2.2 FAA Long-Range Radars (ARSR-4, CRSR)
- **Owner/Operator:** FAA / DOD (dual-use for homeland defense and air traffic control)
- **Description:** Long-range radar systems with detection range of 200–250 nautical miles and altitude detection up to 100,000 feet. ARSR-4 systems are equally spaced around the U.S. perimeter; CRSR systems are located in the U.S. interior.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Provides en-route surveillance coverage at higher altitudes. Can potentially detect UAP at altitude over broad areas. Used for both air traffic control and national defense/homeland security.
- **Limitations:** Line-of-sight limitations due to Earth's curvature. Western U.S. has screening from mountains. Target size assumptions (1 sq meter) affect detection range. Fixed-face, constant search mode — not taskable.
- **Who referenced it:** Mike Freie

### 2.3 ADS-B (Automatic Dependent Surveillance-Broadcast)
- **Owner/Operator:** FAA
- **Description:** Cooperative surveillance system where aircraft avionics broadcast their position, altitude, speed, and other data. Primary data source for air traffic management. Provides very good coverage across the U.S. down to 1,500 feet AGL.
- **Relevance to UAP:** By definition, cooperative surveillance data is "neither unknown nor anomalous" — aircraft broadcasting ADS-B are identified. However, ADS-B data is critical for cross-referencing: objects detected on non-cooperative radar can be matched against ADS-B tracks to identify known aircraft (as in the P-3 three-object case).
- **Limitations:** Only detects cooperative (cooperating) aircraft. Does not detect drones, balloons, or non-equipped aircraft. Not useful for detecting truly unknown or anomalous objects directly.
- **Who referenced it:** Mike Freie

### 2.4 FAA ERAM System (En Route Automation Modernization)
- **Owner/Operator:** FAA
- **Description:** The automation system used in en route air traffic control centers that processes and displays radar track data to controllers for the en route environment.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Processed radar data displayed on ERAM is what is retained for archival purposes. Represents the processed (filtered) form of radar data available for post-event analysis.
- **Limitations:** Retains processed data (what was displayed to controllers), not raw I/Q data. Raw radar data is huge in volume and cost-prohibitive to store. Filter settings may remove small or slow-moving targets.
- **Who referenced it:** Mike Freie

### 2.5 FAA STAR System (Standard Terminal Automation Replacement)
- **Owner/Operator:** FAA
- **Description:** Terminal-area automation system used at airport facilities for processing and displaying radar data in the terminal environment.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Similar to ERAM but for terminal areas. Processed data from STAR is what would be available for retrospective analysis of UAP events near airports.
- **Limitations:** Same as ERAM — processed data only, not raw radar. Subject to filtering that removes small/slow targets.
- **Who referenced it:** Mike Freie

### 2.6 FAA Domestic Events Network
- **Owner/Operator:** FAA
- **Description:** Internal FAA reporting network/organization where UAP sightings by air traffic controllers are filed and archived.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Serves as the archival repository for FAA UAP reports. Data is presumably shared with AARO's database. Historically receives 3–5 reports per month across all 14,000+ controllers.
- **Limitations:** Very low reporting rate relative to total operations. Reporting depends on controller willingness to file reports. Unclear how fully data integrates with AARO holdings.
- **Who referenced it:** Mike Freie

### 2.7 FAA UAP Reporting Protocol (Air Traffic Controller Order)
- **Owner/Operator:** FAA
- **Description:** Formal procedure within the air traffic controller order that defines how controllers report UAP sightings or events. Controllers who see something they cannot identify can report through this established process.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Primary mechanism for civilian aviation UAP reporting. Saw uptick from 3–5/month to 8–10/month (August 2022, possibly Starlink-related), then to 68+ reports starting February 2023 (Chinese balloon incident).
- **Limitations:** Reporting is voluntary (not mandated). Subject to stigma. Reports are narrative-based rather than sensor-based. Does not capture sensor data, only human observations.
- **Who referenced it:** Mike Freie, Mike Gold

## 3. Space-Based Assets

### 3.1 Earth Sensing Satellites (NASA/NOAA)
- **Owner/Operator:** NASA / NOAA
- **Description:** Satellites observing Earth's surface, atmosphere, and oceans. Include weather, land imaging, and atmospheric monitoring satellites with various spectral and spatial resolutions.
- **Relevance to UAP:** AARO is evaluating whether earth-sensing satellites can detect UAP. These platforms provide persistent, calibrated observations from space. Could potentially capture anomalous objects against Earth's background.
- **Limitations:** Not designed or calibrated for UAP detection. Spatial resolution, frame rate, and spectral coverage may not be optimized for detecting small, fast-moving objects. Data was collected for other purposes, introducing bias.
- **Who referenced it:** Sean Kirkpatrick, Nicola Fox

### 3.2 Spy Satellites (Classified)
- **Owner/Operator:** Intelligence Community (e.g., NRO)
- **Description:** Classified reconnaissance satellites with high-resolution imaging and other collection capabilities.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Could potentially image UAP objects from space, but data is classified. David Spergel noted: "If a spy satellite takes an image of a balloon, that data is classified" due to desire to protect technical capabilities.
- **Limitations:** All data is classified. Cannot be used for open scientific analysis. Classification protects technical capabilities from adversarial nations.
- **Who referenced it:** David Spergel

### 3.3 Hubble Space Telescope
- **Owner/Operator:** NASA / ESA
- **Description:** Space-based optical telescope operating in visible, ultraviolet, and near-infrared wavelengths. Used as an example of well-calibrated scientific instruments.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Referenced as an example of how well-characterized scientific instruments produce data artifacts (saturation, bleeding, ghosting) that can create unusual-looking phenomena. Illustrates the importance of understanding instrument behavior.
- **Limitations:** Designed for deep-space astronomy, not atmospheric observation. Pointing at Earth or the Sun would destroy detectors. Not applicable for direct UAP detection.
- **Who referenced it:** David Spergel

### 3.4 James Webb Space Telescope
- **Owner/Operator:** NASA / ESA / CSA
- **Description:** Infrared space telescope with advanced detectors. Referenced for its data characteristics and artifacts.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Used as an example of how even the most advanced scientific instruments produce unexpected artifacts and phenomena in their data. Demonstrates the principle that understanding instrument behavior is essential before claiming anomalous detections.
- **Limitations:** Designed for infrared astronomy of distant objects, not for atmospheric or near-Earth observations. Not suitable for UAP detection.
- **Who referenced it:** David Spergel

### 3.5 International Space Station (ISS)
- **Owner/Operator:** NASA / International Partners
- **Description:** Orbital research platform in low Earth orbit. Used for high-speed imaging of atmospheric phenomena including sprites (upward-going lightning).
- **Relevance to UAP:** ISS has been used to capture high-speed imaging data of ionospheric phenomena like sprites, which were initially seen by commercial pilots and not believed. Demonstrates how space-based platforms can characterize atmospheric phenomena relevant to UAP misidentification.
- **Limitations:** Orbital mechanics limit persistent observation of any single location. Astronauts' visual observations are subject to optical illusions (atmospheric lensing, perspective effects).
- **Who referenced it:** David Spergel, Scott Kelly

### 3.6 NASA Earth Science Satellite Fleet
- **Owner/Operator:** NASA
- **Description:** Comprehensive fleet of Earth-observing satellites collecting atmospheric, oceanic, land, and climate data. Over 60 years of accumulated data and expertise in measuring phenomena in air and space.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Tremendous archive of freely and openly available data. Could be analyzed for anomalous events using AI/ML tools. Provides environmental context (weather, ionospheric conditions) for UAP event analysis. Long-term time series enable characterization of "normal."
- **Limitations:** Data was not collected for UAP detection purposes. Sensors have specific spectral/spatial/temporal resolutions optimized for Earth science, not UAP detection. Frame rates may be too low for fast-moving objects.
- **Who referenced it:** Nicola Fox, Paula Bontempi

### 3.7 Commercial Satellite Constellations
- **Owner/Operator:** Commercial Space Companies
- **Description:** Growing constellation of commercial Earth observation and communications satellites providing persistent collection capabilities.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Provide persistent, ongoing collection that could potentially detect anomalous objects. Some companies lobbied to present to the panel. Well-calibrated systems but collected for different purposes.
- **Limitations:** Data collected for commercial purposes, not UAP detection. Proprietary data access may be limited. Systems are "only one source of many that could be used."
- **Who referenced it:** Nicola Fox (Jen), Nicola Fox

## 4. Ground-Based Scientific Instruments

### 4.1 Large-Scale Ground-Based Scientific Instrumentation
- **Owner/Operator:** Various (Academia, NSF, DOE, international partners)
- **Description:** Broad category of large scientific instruments including radio telescopes, particle detectors, and other research facilities not designed for UAP detection but potentially capable of detecting anomalous signatures.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Sean Kirkpatrick recommended NASA lead evaluation of whether these instruments could detect UAP signatures. The FRB/microwave oven example demonstrated that instruments not designed for a purpose can still detect unexpected signals. Could serve as "tip and cue" systems.
- **Limitations:** Not designed for UAP detection. May not have the right sensitivity, field of view, or temporal resolution. Would need modeling and simulation to determine if the UAP target package is detectable.
- **Who referenced it:** Sean Kirkpatrick, Federica Bianco

### 4.2 Astrophysical & Geophysical Observatories
- **Owner/Operator:** Academic Institutions / NSF / DOE / NASA
- **Description:** Ground-based observatories for astronomy and geophysics, including optical telescopes, radio telescopes, and geophysical monitoring stations. Some modern observatories are specifically designed for time-domain astronomy and detection of rapidly moving objects (near-Earth objects, asteroids, meteors).
- **Relevance to UAP:** Could be leveraged for UAP detection given their capabilities for detecting moving objects and transient phenomena. Multi-platform, multi-site observations from ground and space are already standard practice in astrophysics. The 2023 decadal survey explicitly recommended agencies share data and facilities.
- **Limitations:** Point away from Earth (designed for deep-space observation). Frame rates may not be sufficient for fast-moving atmospheric objects. Would need to be recalibrated or repurposed for atmospheric monitoring.
- **Who referenced it:** Federica Bianco, Shelley Wright

### 4.3 Near-Earth Object Detection Facilities
- **Owner/Operator:** NASA / Academic Institutions
- **Description:** Ground-based and space-based facilities designed to detect and track near-Earth objects such as asteroids, meteors, and potentially interstellar objects (like 'Oumuamua).
- **Relevance to UAP:** David Grinspoon recommended leveraging these capabilities to search for objects in the solar system with anomalous motion, trajectories, light curves, or spectral signatures. Could detect extraterrestrial artifacts or interstellar objects.
- **Limitations:** Most of the solar system has not been searched for artifacts or anomalies. Facilities are optimized for specific types of objects, not general anomaly detection.
- **Who referenced it:** Federica Bianco, David Grinspoon

### 4.4 Fast Radio Burst (FRB) Observatories
- **Owner/Operator:** Academic Institutions (including Australian observatory)
- **Description:** Radio telescope facilities that detected Fast Radio Bursts — powerful explosions at cosmological distances. Used as a case study for how instruments not optimized for a discovery can still make it.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Key analogy: FRBs were discovered by sensitive instruments not optimized for that purpose. Some bursts were real (cosmological explosions), some were terrestrial (microwave oven interference). Demonstrates the principle of serendipitous detection and the importance of understanding terrestrial interference sources.
- **Limitations:** Designed for radio astronomy, not atmospheric monitoring. Susceptible to terrestrial radio frequency interference (the microwave oven problem).
- **Who referenced it:** David Spergel

### 4.5 Radiological Sensors
- **Owner/Operator:** NASA
- **Description:** Sensors capable of detecting radiological signatures in the environment.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Identified by Sean Kirkpatrick as one of NASA's unique capabilities that could assist in UAP detection and characterization. Could detect anomalous radiological signatures associated with UAP events.
- **Limitations:** Not specifically designed or calibrated for UAP detection. Would need evaluation to determine if UAP target signatures are within sensor capabilities.
- **Who referenced it:** Sean Kirkpatrick

### 4.6 Gravitational Wave Detectors
- **Owner/Operator:** NSF / LIGO Scientific Collaboration
- **Description:** Extremely sensitive instruments (e.g., LIGO) designed to detect gravitational waves from cosmic events.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Listed as one of NASA's tools that could potentially contribute to UAP characterization. Represents the class of fundamental physics instruments that could detect anomalous physical signatures.
- **Limitations:** Designed for specific astrophysical phenomena. Extremely specialized sensitivity. Not clear how they would relate to atmospheric UAP observations.
- **Who referenced it:** Sean Kirkpatrick

### 4.7 Geomagnetic Wave Detectors
- **Owner/Operator:** NASA / NOAA
- **Description:** Instruments monitoring Earth's geomagnetic field for variations and anomalies.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Identified as a NASA capability that could assist in UAP detection. Anomalous objects might produce detectable geomagnetic signatures.
- **Limitations:** Designed for geophysical monitoring, not UAP detection. Would need evaluation of whether UAP phenomenology produces detectable geomagnetic signals.
- **Who referenced it:** Sean Kirkpatrick

### 4.8 National Weather Service Radiosondes / Weather Balloons
- **Owner/Operator:** NOAA / National Weather Service
- **Description:** Weather balloons launched from 92 weather stations twice daily (0000Z and 1200Z), reaching up to 100,000 feet with approximately 2-hour flight duration. At least 184 launches daily in CONUS, plus university and hobbyist launches.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Major source of background "clutter" in UAP observations. Weather balloons are a common source of UAP misidentification. Their known flight paths and tracking data are critical for ruling out mundane explanations. NWS coordinates with air traffic control before launches.
- **Limitations:** As a "data source" for UAP, they are primarily a confounding factor rather than a detection tool. Their presence complicates UAP analysis. Commercial and hobbyist balloons may not be tracked as carefully.
- **Who referenced it:** Mike Freie, Nadia Drake

## 5. Crowdsource/Public Data

### 5.1 iPhone / Smartphone Sensors
- **Owner/Operator:** Consumer / Apple (and other manufacturers)
- **Description:** Consumer smartphones with cameras, GPS, accelerometers, and other sensors. Provide location, speed, imagery, and ancillary metadata.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Sean Kirkpatrick noted that "imagery from an iPhone is generally not helpful unless you are right up on whatever it is you're looking at." However, ancillary data (location, speed, timestamp, other phenomenologies) from multiple phones can be very helpful. David Spergel proposed NASA could develop an app for standardized data collection from ~3–4 billion smartphones worldwide.
- **Limitations:** Camera imagery alone is generally low quality for UAP analysis. Multiple cameras needed to eliminate optical artifacts like ghosting. Data is uncalibrated and poorly characterized. Requires a platform for standardized collection with metadata.
- **Who referenced it:** Sean Kirkpatrick, David Spergel, Walter (Scott) Randolph

### 5.2 Citizen Science Data Platform (Proposed)
- **Owner/Operator:** NASA (proposed)
- **Description:** A proposed platform to crowdsource UAP data collection from the public, supporting collection of data and metadata, transmission to a central repository, and coordination among observers for follow-up observations.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Would enable standardized, multi-sensor data collection with proper metadata (sensor type, noise characteristics, time, sensitivity, environmental conditions). Could enable rapid follow-up when anomalies are detected. Would connect observers so multiple sensors can be pointed at a reported event.
- **Limitations:** Does not yet exist. Would need to reach a large enough community to be effective. Requires coordination infrastructure. Challenge of ensuring data quality from non-expert observers.
- **Who referenced it:** Federica Bianco, David Spergel

### 5.3 Eyewitness Reports
- **Owner/Operator:** Public / Pilots / Military Aviators
- **Description:** Narrative accounts from individuals who have observed anomalous phenomena. Include reports from commercial pilots, military aviators, and general public.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Constitute the bulk of existing UAP data. May contain important information such as persistent sighting locations and seasonality. Military aviator reports (especially Navy) provide the most detailed contextual information. Cannot alone ascertain the nature of UAPs but are valuable when combined with sensor data.
- **Limitations:** On their own, cannot provide conclusive evidence. Often muddled, lack quality control and data curation. Subject to optical illusions, cognitive bias, and misperception. Commercial pilots are "very reluctant to report anomalies" due to stigma. Background objects (commercial aircraft, balloons, weather) can mimic UAP.
- **Who referenced it:** David Spergel, Nicola Fox, Nadia Drake, Federica Bianco

### 5.4 Social Media / Google Search Trends
- **Owner/Operator:** Various Platforms (Google, etc.)
- **Description:** Public social media posts and search engine query data that could serve as real-time indicators of UAP sightings or events in specific areas.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Proposed as a potential "tip and cue" mechanism — similar to how Google search surges can indicate disease outbreaks. Could signal where something is happening and enable rapid sensor tasking in that area.
- **Limitations:** Not scientific data. Subject to misinformation and false reports. Would require real-time processing capability. Federica Bianco cautioned about the limitations of using such data for scientific analysis.
- **Who referenced it:** Josh Sumeta (Speaker 4)

### 5.5 Nonprofit/For-Profit UAP Tracking Organizations
- **Owner/Operator:** Various NGOs and Commercial Companies
- **Description:** Organizations that track and catalog UAP sightings, including both nonprofit groups and for-profit companies. Some lobbied to present to the panel.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Maintain troves of UAP-related data. Panel reviewed their inputs but concluded much of the collected data is "not considered scientific data in nature" — not unbiased, not repeatable, and typically comes with eyewitness accounts rather than sensor data.
- **Limitations:** Data does not meet FAIR standards. Not unbiased. Not repeatable. Typically lacks quantitative sensor data. Companies' data is collected for purposes other than UAP. Systems, while well-calibrated, are "only one source of many."
- **Who referenced it:** Nicola Fox (Jen)

## 6. Databases/Archives

### 6.1 NASA Open Data Portal (data.nasa.gov)
- **Owner/Operator:** NASA
- **Description:** Public portal providing free access to tens of thousands of NASA datasets across all mission areas. Fully accessible to the public.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Primary access point for NASA data that could be analyzed for UAP research. Contains Earth science, heliophysics, and other data that could provide environmental context for UAP events.
- **Limitations:** Data was not collected for UAP purposes. Users must navigate the data themselves to find potentially relevant datasets. Not all data is equally useful for UAP analysis.
- **Who referenced it:** Nicola Fox

### 6.2 Data.gov
- **Owner/Operator:** U.S. Government (GSA)
- **Description:** U.S. government's open data portal providing access to federal agency data archives, including science and mission data from NASA and other agencies.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Provides overview and access to archives of science and mission data from multiple agencies that could be relevant to UAP analysis.

## Key Cross-Cutting Themes

### Data Quality Challenges
Throughout the meeting, a consistent theme emerged: **the need for high-quality, well-calibrated, multi-sensor data**. Current UAP data is described as:
- Unsystematic and fragmented across agencies
- Uncalibrated for scientific data collection
- Poorly characterized and not well curated
- Insufficient to draw scientific conclusions

### The "Haystack" Problem
Multiple speakers used the analogy of finding an anomaly in a complex environment:
- **45,000** FAA flights per day in U.S. airspace
- **184+** weather balloon launches daily in CONUS
- **880,000** registered commercial drones
- **1.69 million** recreational/model UAS
- Only **2–5%** of AARO's 800+ cases display potentially anomalous signatures

### Classification Barrier
A fundamental tension exists between national security classification and scientific openness. Defense and intelligence sensor data on UAP is typically classified — not because of what is observed, but because of the sensor capabilities used to observe it. This prevents open scientific analysis of the most capable sensor data.

### Multi-Sensor Imperative
Repeated emphasis that no single sensor or data source is sufficient. Proper UAP characterization requires:
- Multiple sensor types (EO, IR, radar, etc.)
- Multiple platforms (ground, air, space)
- Multiple perspectives to eliminate optical artifacts
- Correlation with known background objects (ADS-B tracks, weather balloon positions, etc.)
