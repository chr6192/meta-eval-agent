## Detailed Source Descriptions

---

### Government/Military Sensors

#### 1. DOD Sensors (Classified)

- **Owner/Operator:** U.S. Department of Defense
- **Description:** A broad category of sensors across the Department of Defense used for national security, defense, and intelligence collection. Data collected is often classified, not because of what is observed, but because of the sensor platform itself.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Sean Kirkpatrick (AARO) and Nicola Fox (NASA) both emphasized that most existing UAP reports come from DOD sensor platforms. AARO is tasked with evaluating these classified sensors against known UAP target characteristics. Kirkpatrick noted that DOD sensors are "not scientific sensors" — they are designed "to identify an object that is known and put a weapon on it." A major effort underway is to calibrate these sensors against known objects (e.g., flying F-35s past weather balloons) to establish baselines.
- **Limitations:** Data is classified, limiting scientific community access. Sensors are not calibrated for scientific measurement; designed for targeting. Cannot be pointed arbitrarily due to legal restrictions on surveillance of civilian areas within the continental U.S.
- **Referenced by:** Sean Kirkpatrick, Nicola Fox, David Spergel

---

#### 2. F-35 Stealth Fighter Sensors

- **Owner/Operator:** U.S. Air Force / U.S. Navy
- **Description:** Advanced electro-optical, infrared, and radar sensors aboard the F-35 fifth-generation stealth fighter aircraft.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Used as an example by Nicola Fox to illustrate how data can be classified due to the platform rather than the subject — "if a camera on an F-35 took a picture of a bird, it's classified." Sean Kirkpatrick referenced using F-35s in calibration exercises against known targets such as weather balloons under various conditions (speeds, altitudes, lighting, heating) to establish sensor baselines.
- **Limitations:** Classified data; not a scientific sensor. Sensor calibration for UAP purposes is still in progress.
- **Referenced by:** Nicola Fox, Sean Kirkpatrick

---

#### 3. MQ-9 Reaper EO Sensors

- **Owner/Operator:** U.S. Air Force
- **Description:** Electro-Optical (EO) imaging sensors mounted on the MQ-9 Reaper unmanned aerial system (drone).
- **Relevance to UAP:** Sean Kirkpatrick presented a case of a spherical, metallic orb detected in the Middle East in 2022 by an MQ-9. The object showed interesting apparent maneuvers but demonstrated "no enigmatic technical capabilities and was no threat to airborne safety." Kirkpatrick confirmed the object was real and not a sensor artifact.
- **Limitations:** Single-sensor observation; lacks corroborating multi-sensor data. Limited ability to determine true size, speed, or altitude without ranging data. Walter Scott questioned whether sensor artifacts or data processing artifacts were fully ruled out.
- **Referenced by:** Sean Kirkpatrick, Walter Scott

---

#### 4. P-3 Orion Aircraft Sensors

- **Owner/Operator:** U.S. Navy
- **Description:** Sensors aboard the P-3 Orion maritime patrol aircraft, including stabilized/infrared imaging cameras.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Sean Kirkpatrick presented a newly released video showing three dots moving back and forth over the ocean, initially reported as a grouping of three UAP. The P-3 attempted to intercept but was unable to catch them. Subsequent analysis matching the objects against FAA air traffic control data revealed they were actually commercial aircraft on a major flight corridor, approximately 30–40 miles away. The apparent anomalous motion was caused by sensor/platform jitter, not the objects themselves.
- **Limitations:** Demonstrated the severe problem of misperception — even "very highly trained pilots" can be fooled by parallax and sensor jitter. Single-sensor data without range information led to initial misinterpretation.
- **Referenced by:** Sean Kirkpatrick, Nicola Fox

---

#### 5. Navy Aviator FLIR + Ranging (Pilot-Airborne Targeting System)

- **Owner/Operator:** U.S. Navy
- **Description:** Combination of Forward Looking Infrared (FLIR) imaging and ranging systems on Navy aircraft, providing elevation angle, azimuth, target range in nautical miles, aircraft altitude, time reference, and indicated airspeed — all displayed to pilots.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Josh Sumeta (NASA panel) presented the "Go Fast" case — a 2015 video recorded from the USS Theodore Roosevelt showing an object that appeared to move rapidly over the ocean. Using the multi-sensor data on the display (range, angle, aircraft altitude, time), he applied trigonometry to determine the object was at ~13,000 ft altitude, ~4.2 miles from the ocean surface, and moving at only ~40 mph — consistent with wind speed at that altitude. The apparent "fast" motion was entirely due to the aircraft's own motion (~430 mph) and parallax effect.
- **Limitations:** Range information has associated uncertainties. Requires multiple data points over time for reliable velocity determination. Josh Sumeta emphasized that the Navy aviator reports with the most detailed contextual information are the ones with this multi-sensor capability.
- **Referenced by:** Josh Sumeta, Karlin Toner

---

#### 6. Tomcat Fighter (AV-8B) Sensors

- **Owner/Operator:** U.S. Marine Corps / U.S. Navy
- **Description:** Avionics and visual observation from Tomcat fighter aircraft.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Scott Kelly (former NASA astronaut, Navy aviator) recounted an incident where he and his Radar Intercept Officer (RIO) flew through a military operating area off Virginia Beach. The RIO was "convinced we flew by a UFO," but Kelly didn't see it. When they turned around to investigate, it turned out to be a Bart Simpson weather balloon. Illustrates how the aerial/space environment is "very, very conducive to optical illusions."
- **Limitations:** Relies heavily on human visual interpretation, prone to optical illusions and misperception. Sensors have "the same issues as the people's eyeballs."
- **Referenced by:** Scott Kelly

---

#### 7. AARO Purpose-Built Sensors

- **Owner/Operator:** All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO)
- **Description:** Sensors purpose-built by AARO specifically to detect, track, and characterize objects matching the typical UAP target profile derived from reported cases. These are being deployed in select areas for surveillance.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Sean Kirkpatrick described these as part of AARO's scientific plan. After evaluating existing sensors against the "typical UAP target," AARO is augmenting with dedicated sensors designed for the specific detection task. This includes a "bootstrap method" — starting with broad spectrum search across few indicators, then refining and narrowing sensors based on findings.
- **Limitations:** Still in deployment; described as a "bootstrap method" initially. Limited number of sensors deployed in very select areas.
- **Referenced by:** Sean Kirkpatrick

---

#### 8. Intelligence Community Sensors

- **Owner/Operator:** U.S. Intelligence Community
- **Description:** High-precision, calibrated sensors operated by various elements of the Intelligence Community.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Sean Kirkpatrick distinguished IC sensors from DOD sensors: "Believe it or not, intelligence community sensors are very close to scientific sensors. They are calibrated, they are high precision, they are everything you'd ever want to know about a thing." These are being evaluated alongside DOD, commercial, and civilian sensors for their ability to detect UAP target profiles.
- **Limitations:** Classified data. Access restricted to cleared personnel.
- **Referenced by:** Sean Kirkpatrick

---

#### 9. F-15 / F-18 Fighter Radar Cross-Section

- **Owner/Operator:** U.S. Air Force / U.S. Navy
- **Description:** Fourth-generation fighter aircraft used as reference targets for radar cross-section characterization. An F-15 or F-18 has a radar cross-section of approximately 1 square meter.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Mike Freie (FAA) used the F-15/F-18 as a reference point when discussing what sizes of targets can be detected by FAA radar systems. He noted that an F-15 is ~1 m², a large airliner is ~100 m², and a small drone is ~0.01 m² — providing context for detection capabilities relative to different target sizes.
- **Limitations:** Used as a calibration reference, not as a direct UAP detection system.
- **Referenced by:** Mike Freie

### Civilian Aviation Systems

#### 10. FAA Short-Range Radars (ASR — Airport Surveillance Radar)

- **Owner/Operator:** Federal Aviation Administration
- **Description:** Short-range radar systems located at high and medium-density airports with a detection range of 40–60 miles and altitude detection capability of about 24,000 feet.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Mike Freie described these as part of the FAA's non-cooperative surveillance capability. The detection models assume a target of 1 square meter (~1 meter diameter sphere). The radars are fixed-face and rotate at either 12 RPM or 5 RPM depending on en-route or terminal surveillance requirements.
- **Limitations:** Limited range (40–60 miles). Limited altitude coverage (up to ~24,000 ft). Coverage is denser in the eastern U.S. (more airports/population) and sparser in the west. Subject to terrain screening, particularly in mountainous western areas. Designed to filter out biologicals (insects, dust) and ground clutter, which may miss small anomalous targets.
- **Referenced by:** Mike Freie

---

#### 11. FAA Long-Range Radars (ARSR-4)

- **Owner/Operator:** Federal Aviation Administration
- **Description:** Air Route Surveillance Radar (ARSR-4) systems located around the perimeter of the continental United States, equally spaced. Detection range of 200–250 nautical miles with altitude detection up to 100,000 feet. Used for both air traffic control and national defense/homeland security.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Provides coverage of the en-route environment where many UAP reports occur. Mike Freie noted good coverage across the U.S. at 10,000 feet and above. These are non-cooperative (radar-based) sensors capable of detecting objects not transmitting transponder signals.
- **Limitations:** Line-of-sight limitation due to Earth curvature reduces detection range for low-altitude targets (e.g., at 1,000 ft AGL, detection range is significantly reduced). Terrain screening affects western U.S. coverage. Data is retained in processed form for months but is cycled over time. Not all raw radar data is preserved.
- **Referenced by:** Mike Freie, David Spergel

---

#### 12. FAA Long-Range Radars (CRSR — Combat Rescue Surveillance Radar)

- **Owner/Operator:** Federal Aviation Administration
- **Description:** Combat Rescue Surveillance Radar (CRSR) systems located on the interior of the United States, providing long-range detection similar to ARSR-4 systems.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Part of the FAA's long-range surveillance network for both air traffic control and homeland defense. Provides inland coverage complementing the perimeter ARSR-4 installations.
- **Limitations:** Same limitations as ARSR-4 regarding altitude coverage, terrain screening, and data retention.
- **Referenced by:** Mike Freie

---

#### 13. ADS-B (Automatic Dependent Surveillance Broadcast)

- **Owner/Operator:** Federal Aviation Administration
- **Description:** A cooperative surveillance system where aircraft broadcast their position, altitude, speed, and identity via GPS-based avionics, received by ground stations and other aircraft.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Mike Freie described ADS-B as "a primary data source and a preferred data source for the management of the airspace system." It provides very good coverage across the U.S. down to 1,500 feet above ground level for equipped aircraft. However, Freie noted that "from the standpoint of cooperative sensors, those are neither unknown nor anomalous" — meaning ADS-B-equipped objects are by definition known and identified.
- **Limitations:** Only detects cooperative (equipped) aircraft. UAPs by definition would not be transmitting ADS-B signals. 880,000 registered drones in the U.S., many operating without transponders.
- **Referenced by:** Mike Freie

---

#### 14. FAA Domestic Event Network (DEN)

- **Owner/Operator:** Federal Aviation Administration
- **Description:** The FAA's internal reporting and communication network for aviation events, including UAP sightings. Air traffic controllers report UAP observations through this system.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Mike Freie and Mike Gold discussed that UAP reports from controllers flow into the DEN and are archived. Mike Gold questioned whether these reports feed into AARO's database. Reports historically average 3–5 per month across all ~14,000 FAA controllers, with upticks during the August 2022 Starlink launches (8–10/month) and the February 2023 Chinese balloon incident (68 reports in February).
- **Limitations:** Very low reporting rate (3–5/month from 14,000 controllers). Possible stigma inhibiting reporting. Mike Freie stated there is a reporting process defined in FAA orders but was not aware of "any specific stigma or limitations" beyond that.
- **Referenced by:** Mike Freie, Mike Gold, Karlin Toner

---

#### 15. FAA UAP Reporting Process

- **Owner/Operator:** Federal Aviation Administration
- **Description:** The formal process by which FAA air traffic controllers report UAP sightings or events, defined in an FAA order governing controller procedures.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Mike Freie described that controllers are instructed: "If you see something, here's the procedure by which you would report it." For civilian pilots, Mike Gold asked about the reporting process, but Mike Freie admitted he didn't know the process from a pilot perspective — only the controller process.
- **Limitations:** Process exists for controllers but not clearly accessible or well-known for civilian pilots. Potential stigma may limit reporting even where process exists.
- **Referenced by:** Mike Freie, Mike Gold, Karlin Toner

### Space-Based Assets

#### 16. NASA Earth Science Satellites

- **Owner/Operator:** NASA
- **Description:** Satellites in Earth orbit dedicated to measuring and monitoring Earth's atmosphere, oceans, land, and climate. Examples include those measuring atmospheric phenomena, oceanic conditions, and terrestrial changes.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Sean Kirkpatrick recommended that NASA evaluate whether any earth-sensing satellites could detect the typical UAP target profile. Dr. Paula Bontempi emphasized that NASA has "over 60 years of experience measuring phenomena in air and space" using the "unique vantage point of space." Multiple panelists suggested NASA should convene a task force to assess which current assets could provide insight into UAP targets.
- **Limitations:** Not designed for UAP detection. Spatial resolution, spectral resolution, and frame rates may not be sufficient to detect small, fast-moving objects. Walter Scott noted NASA's current assets would primarily see "really big haystacks that are moving very slowly." Frame rate is a major concern — detecting fast-moving objects requires rapid imaging.
- **Referenced by:** Sean Kirkpatrick, Dr. Paula Bontempi, Shelley Wright, Walter Scott

---

#### 17. NASA Earth Observing Satellites

- **Owner/Operator:** NASA
- **Description:** Broader category of NASA's satellite constellation for Earth observation, overlapping with Earth science satellites but encompassing all NASA orbital platforms with Earth-facing sensors.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Referenced as part of the "tremendous archive of NASA data" from "satellites and other space-based and ground networks" that is freely and openly available to the public. Dr. Bontempi suggested these could be leveraged to study environmental conditions around the time of reported UAP events.
- **Limitations:** Same as above — not purpose-built for UAP detection. Frame rate and resolution may be inadequate for small, fast-moving targets.
- **Referenced by:** Dr. Paula Bontempi, Nicola Fox

---

#### 18. James Webb Space Telescope (JWST)

- **Owner/Operator:** NASA / ESA / CSA
- **Description:** NASA's premier infrared space telescope, the most powerful space telescope ever built.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Referenced by David Spergel to illustrate the importance of well-calibrated instruments. He noted that JWST and Hubble can produce "really at first funny phenomenon" — such as detector saturation bleeding, ghosting, and diffused images from light bouncing off dust in the telescope — which could be mistaken for anomalies. The point: before claiming an anomaly, one must understand the instrument thoroughly.
- **Limitations:** Not designed for UAP detection. Pointed at space, not Earth's atmosphere. The telescope is extremely sensitive to stray light.
- **Referenced by:** David Spergel

---

#### 19. Hubble Space Telescope (HST)

- **Owner/Operator:** NASA / ESA
- **Description:** NASA's iconic space-based optical telescope in low Earth orbit.
- **Relevance to UAP:** David Spergel used Hubble as an example of how even the best scientific instruments produce artifacts — "ghosting" from sunlight reflecting off optics, detector saturation from bright stars, and diffraction effects. These are relevant to UAP study because similar optical artifacts from uncalibrated or misused instruments may be misidentified as anomalous phenomena. Spergel noted you would "never take the Hubble Telescope and point it at the sun" because it would destroy the detectors, but even indirect sunlight can produce "very strange images."
- **Limitations:** Not designed for UAP detection. Highly specialized for astronomical observation.
- **Referenced by:** David Spergel

---

#### 20. International Space Station (ISS)

- **Owner/Operator:** NASA / International Partners (ESA, JAXA, CSA, Roscosmos)
- **Description:** Permanently crewed space station in low Earth orbit.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Scott Kelly recounted an incident where astronauts preparing to close the Space Shuttle payload bay doors during STS-124 (2008) saw an object they thought was a tool or bolt. A photograph revealed it was actually the ISS itself, approximately 80 miles away — a powerful example of optical illusions in the space environment. Kelly also noted that high-speed imaging data from the ISS has been used to study ionospheric phenomena like sprites (upward-going lightning).
- **Limitations:** Not a systematic UAP detection platform. Crew observations are anecdotal and subject to the same optical illusion effects.
- **Referenced by:** Scott Kelly, David Spergel

---

#### 21. NASA Planetary Missions (Solar System)

- **Owner/Operator:** NASA
- **Description:** Missions exploring the solar system, including probes, orbiters, rovers, and landers studying planets, moons, asteroids, and other bodies.
- **Relevance to UAP:** David Grinspoon proposed that NASA could leverage its solar system exploration capabilities to search for objects in space with anomalous motion, anomalous trajectories, unusual light curves, or anomalous spectral signatures. He argued that "most of the solar system has not been searched for artifacts or anomalies" and that these modest data analysis efforts could be applied to existing and planned missions.
- **Limitations:** Missions are designed for specific planetary science objectives, not anomaly hunting. Would require repurposing analysis pipelines.
- **Referenced by:** David Grinspoon

---

#### 22. Commercial Satellite Systems

- **Owner/Operator:** Private Space Companies (e.g., Planet Labs, Maxar, etc.)
- **Description:** A growing number of commercial satellites providing Earth observation, communications, and other services.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Nicola Fox and Jennifer Buss noted that commercial space companies have "troves of data" collected for various purposes. Several companies lobbied to present to the panel. Dr. Bontempi identified partnerships with private sector as a NASA strength. These systems "are well calibrated" but are "only one source of many" and were not collected for UAP purposes.
- **Limitations:** Data collected for commercial purposes (Earth imaging, weather, communications), not for UAP detection. Access to data may require commercial arrangements.
- **Referenced by:** Nicola Fox, Dr. Paula Bontempi, Jennifer Buss

---

#### 23. Starlink Satellite Constellation

- **Owner/Operator:** SpaceX
- **Description:** Low Earth orbit satellite constellation for global internet connectivity, consisting of thousands of satellites.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Mike Freie noted an uptick in FAA UAP reports in August 2022 (from 3–5/month to 8–10/month) that was "perhaps due to Starlink launches." The mass satellite deployments may have been misidentified as anomalous objects by air traffic controllers.
- **Limitations:** Not a UAP detection system. However, the growing number of satellites adds to the "background" of objects in the sky that must be distinguished from truly anomalous phenomena.
- **Referenced by:** Mike Freie

### Ground-Based Scientific Instruments

#### 24. Astrophysical & Geophysical Observatories (Ground)

- **Owner/Operator:** NSF / NASA / Academia / Various Government Agencies
- **Description:** Large-scale ground-based observatories used for astrophysical, atmospheric, and geophysical research. Includes facilities for optical, infrared, radio, and other wavelength observations.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Federica Bianco proposed that "astrophysical and geographical observatories, both on the ground and satellites" could be leveraged for UAP study. Sean Kirkpatrick recommended "large scale, ground-based scientific instrumentation" and asked whether any of these instruments could detect the typical UAP target profile. Dr. Bianco noted some observatories are "specifically designed for the detection of time anomalies" and for "detecting objects that move rapidly in space, near earth objects, asteroids, meteors."
- **Limitations:** Not designed for UAP detection. Pointed skyward for astronomical observation rather than scanning the lower atmosphere. May have pointing restrictions.
- **Referenced by:** Federica Bianco, Sean Kirkpatrick

---

#### 25. Time-Domain / Transient Astronomy Facilities

- **Owner/Operator:** NSF / NASA / Academia
- **Description:** Modern observatories specifically designed to detect transient astronomical events — objects that appear, change, or disappear on short timescales. These include facilities for detecting supernovae, gamma-ray bursts, gravitational wave counterparts, and fast-moving solar system objects.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Federica Bianco noted that "some modern observatories are designed for the detection of time anomalies specifically, as well as for the detection of objects that move rapidly in space." These could be repurposed or leveraged for UAP study given their high-speed detection capabilities.
- **Limitations:** Designed for deep space, not lower atmosphere. Not optimized for the specific UAP target profile.
- **Referenced by:** Federica Bianco

---

#### 26. Fast Radio Burst (FRB) Observatories

- **Owner/Operator:** University / Government (including an Australian observatory)
- **Description:** Radio telescope arrays designed to detect Fast Radio Bursts — brief, intense bursts of radio emission from unknown cosmic sources.
- **Relevance to UAP:** David Spergel used the FRB discovery story as an analogy for UAP research. Early FRB observations by an Australian observatory picked up strange signals that turned out to be caused by researchers opening microwave oven doors before the microwave stopped, producing radio interference. This illustrates that instruments can pick up anomalous signals, but careful analysis is needed to distinguish real phenomena from mundane sources. Spergel used this to emphasize the need to understand both the instruments and the "background" before claiming anomalies.
- **Limitations:** Used as an illustrative analogy, not proposed as a UAP detection system.
- **Referenced by:** David Spergel

---

#### 27. SETI Radio / Optical Detection Systems

- **Owner/Operator:** SETI Institute / NASA / Partner Organizations
- **Description:** Radio and optical detection systems used in the Search for Extraterrestrial Intelligence (SETI) to detect potential signals or technosignatures from intelligent civilizations.
- **Relevance to UAP:** David Grinspoon discussed the relevance of SETI to UAP research. SETI researchers have "expertise in methodologies for determining whether a potential technosignature is in fact a natural phenomenon or misinterpreted terrestrial technology." The SETI community has developed techniques for assessing whether an anomalous observation "is a real signal, is a known or unknown natural phenomena, must it be technological, is it known terrestrial technology." These methods are relevant to UAP analysis.
- **Limitations:** The "technosignatures" field is relatively new; the classical SETI radio search has well-standardized data, but the broader technosignatures field (looking for unintended technological signatures) has less data history. Stigma has historically limited NASA's engagement with technosignature research.
- **Referenced by:** David Grinspoon, Anamaria Berea

---

#### 28. Near-Earth Object (NEO) Detection Facilities

- **Owner/Operator:** NASA / NASA Partner Agencies (e.g., Pan-STARRS, Catalina Sky Survey)
- **Description:** Ground-based and space-based systems designed to detect and track asteroids, comets, and other objects that could potentially impact Earth.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Federica Bianco noted that facilities "specifically designed to detect... near earth objects, asteroids, meteors" could be leveraged for UAP study. Mike Gold referenced the asteroid Apophis, which will pass below geosynchronous satellite altitude, as an example of why improving orbital monitoring has "great ancillary benefits" — data relevant to UAP study could also improve NEO tracking.
- **Limitations:** Designed for objects in deep space, not atmospheric phenomena. Different size scales and velocities than typical UAP reports.
- **Referenced by:** Federica Bianco, Mike Gold

---

#### 29. Radiological Sensors

- **Owner/Operator:** Government Agencies (various)
- **Description:** Sensors that detect and measure radiation, including nuclear, radioactive, and other ionizing radiation sources.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Sean Kirkpatrick mentioned radiological sensors as part of NASA's unique capabilities that "may assist both AARO and NASA in their UAP efforts." These could potentially detect radiation signatures associated with anomalous objects.
- **Limitations:** No specific application to UAP discussed beyond general capability mention. May not be sensitive to relevant signatures if UAPs have no radiological emissions.
- **Referenced by:** Sean Kirkpatrick

---

#### 30. Gravitational Wave Detectors

- **Owner/Operator:** LIGO / NSF / International Partners
- **Description:** Extremely sensitive interferometric detectors (LIGO, Virgo, KAGRA) that measure tiny distortions in spacetime caused by gravitational waves from cataclysmic cosmic events.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Sean Kirkpatrick listed "tools for gravitational wave detection" as part of NASA's unique capabilities that could be relevant to UAP study. No specific application was detailed.
- **Limitations:** Extremely specialized for detecting spacetime distortions from massive cosmic events. Unclear relevance to atmospheric or near-Earth anomalous objects.
- **Referenced by:** Sean Kirkpatrick

---

#### 31. Geomagnetic Wave Detectors

- **Owner/Operator:** NOAA / Government Agencies
- **Description:** Instruments that measure variations in Earth's magnetic field and detect geomagnetic disturbances.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Sean Kirkpatrick listed "geomagnetic wave detection" among NASA's capabilities relevant to UAP study. These could potentially detect magnetic field anomalies associated with some UAP reports.
- **Limitations:** No specific application to UAP was detailed. Background geomagnetic variation from solar activity and other sources creates significant noise.
- **Referenced by:** Sean Kirkpatrick

---

#### 32. NOAA Weather Balloon Tracking

- **Owner/Operator:** National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA)
- **Description:** Network of 92 weather stations that release weather balloons (radiosondes) twice daily at 0000Z and 1200Z. Balloons ascend to ~100,000 feet where the envelope bursts, then descend. Approximately 184 balloon flights daily in the U.S., with ~1,600 worldwide.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Mike Freie and Nadia Drake cited weather balloons as a major source of "background" objects that can be confused with UAP. The Chinese balloon incident in February 2023 led to a significant uptick in FAA UAP reports (68 in one month). Sean Kirkpatrick's test matrix includes calibrating sensors against weather balloons.
- **Limitations:** Balloons are legitimate sources of confusion for UAP detection. The growing number of private and research balloons adds to the "haystack" of known objects that must be distinguished from anomalous phenomena.
- **Referenced by:** Mike Freie, Nadia Drake, Sean Kirkpatrick

---

#### 33. Ground-Based Radars (FAA and Other)

- **Owner/Operator:** FAA / Other Government and Civilian Agencies
- **Description:** Ground-based radar systems beyond the FAA network, including military, homeland defense, weather (NEXRAD), and civilian radar installations.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Sean Kirkpatrick mentioned evaluating "any of these ground radars, whether it's FAA or other" for their ability to detect the typical UAP target. He also noted plans for 24/7 "collection monitoring campaigns in some of these areas for three months at a time" to baseline what is normal.
- **Limitations:** Weather radars and military radars have different capabilities and limitations from FAA ATC radars. Data access varies by system type.
- **Referenced by:** Sean Kirkpatrick

### Crowdsource / Public Data

#### 34. Cell Phone / iPhone Camera Imagery

- **Owner/Operator:** Public / Citizens
- **Description:** Digital photographs and video captured on consumer smartphones, particularly iPhones, which include embedded metadata (GPS location, timestamp, device orientation).
- **Relevance to UAP:** Sean Kirkpatrick stated that "imagery from an iPhone is generally not helpful unless you are right up on whatever it is you're looking at. However, some of the ancillary data that your iPhone provides from location to speed to other phenomenologies and more than one of those can be very helpful." David Spergel proposed that NASA could develop apps to collect data "in a uniform and centralized way" from the ~3.4 billion phones on the planet, noting that multiple camera perspectives can help eliminate optical artifacts like ghosting.
- **Limitations:** Images alone are generally not helpful for UAP analysis without metadata. Camera sensors are not scientifically calibrated. Susceptible to optical artifacts, ghosting, sensor noise, and atmospheric lensing. Data quality varies enormously.
- **Referenced by:** Sean Kirkpatrick, David Spergel

#### 35. Social Media Data

- **Owner/Operator:** Public / Social Media Platforms (Twitter/X, TikTok, YouTube, etc.)
- **Description:** User-generated content including photos, videos, text reports, and trending topics related to UAP sightings.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Referenced by a panel participant who suggested using social media data as a "tip-and-cue" system — similar to how Google Trends surges can identify disease outbreaks, social media activity could help identify where UAP events are occurring, then cue sensors to those areas. This was debated, with Federica Bianco objecting that social media data alone is not sufficient for scientific analysis.
- **Limitations:** Noisy, unreliable data source. Subject to hoaxes, misidentification, and confirmation bias. Cannot be used directly for scientific analysis without corroboration from calibrated sensors.
- **Referenced by:** Anamaria Berea (as questioner), Federica Bianco

#### 36. Proposed NASA Crowdsourcing Platform

- **Owner/Operator:** NASA (proposed by panel)
- **Description:** A yet-to-be-developed platform that would enable the public to report UAP sightings with structured data and metadata collection, potentially via mobile apps. Federica Bianco emphasized this needs to be a proper "platform" rather than just guidelines, capable of receiving data and metadata, transmitting them to a central repository, and enabling coordinated follow-up observations when sightings occur.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Federica Bianco and David Spergel both advocated for a NASA-led crowdsourcing platform. Bianco proposed it should enable: (1) structured data/metadata collection, (2) centralized curation, (3) community connectivity so that when one person sights something, the broader community can point sensors to the sighting, and (4) follow-up observation coordination.
- **Limitations:** Does not yet exist. Significant development effort required. Data quality control challenges. Would likely produce large volumes of mundane reports (commercial aircraft, balloons, drones).
- **Referenced by:** Federica Bianco, David Spergel

#### 37. Eyewitness Reports (Pilots, Public, Military)

- **Owner/Operator:** Multiple Sources (civilian pilots, military aviators, commercial pilots, general public)
- **Description:** Firsthand accounts of UAP sightings from trained and untrained observers.
- **Relevance to UAP:** Repeatedly discussed throughout the meeting. Nadia Drake noted that "eyewitness reports, which on their own can be interesting and compelling, but often lack the information needed to make definitive conclusions about an object's provenance." Federica Bianco elevated this point: eyewitness reports "cannot ascertain the nature of UAPs" but "should be considered because they may contain important information, for example, persistent sighting locations or seasonality." They only work when "joined with quantitative data collected by sensors as well as biophysical and psychophysical assessments of the witness."
- **Limitations:** Subjective, prone to cognitive bias and optical illusions. Insufficient alone for scientific analysis. Stigma deters reporting (especially among commercial pilots). Karlin Toner noted "many scientists and aviators consider the study of UAPs to be fringe, at best."
- **Referenced by:** Nadia Drake, Federica Bianco, Karlin Toner, David Spergel, Sean Kirkpatrick

## Key Themes Across All Sources

1. **Data Quality Crisis:** Nearly every speaker emphasized that the fundamental problem is insufficient high-quality, well-calibrated data. David Spergel's summary: "We need better data, and we need more uniform data."

2. **Calibration Gap:** Military sensors (DOD) are not scientific instruments — they are designed for targeting, not measurement. AARO is actively working to calibrate these sensors against known objects (weather balloons, drones) to establish baselines.

3. **Multi-Sensor Requirement:** Josh Sumeta's analysis demonstrated that single-sensor observations are fundamentally limited. Multi-sensor, multi-platform, multi-site data collection is essential for determining true object parameters (size, speed, altitude).

4. **The "Haystack" Problem:** The sky contains enormous background noise — 45,000 daily flights, 184+ daily weather balloon launches, 880,000+ registered drones — all of which must be characterized to identify the truly anomalous.

5. **Stigma Barrier:** Commercial pilots and scientists remain reluctant to report anomalies. NASA's public engagement and panel composition were explicitly intended to normalize UAP reporting.

6. **AI/ML Requires Standardized Data:** Federica Bianco and others emphasized that machine learning and AI tools cannot be applied to current UAP data because it does not meet FAIR (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable) standards.

7. **Frame Rate Limitation:** Shelley Wright identified frame rate as a critical gap — current NASA assets are optimized for slow-moving, large targets, not small fast-moving objects. Future instruments may need significantly increased frame rates.
